# Pilot skill moves tempo-card prices

**No per-card skill-bracket data was found, so the claim that a card's worth rises with player skill rests on deck-level numbers, qualitative design writing and the project's own paired prices, not on any published card-level table.** The one hard skill gradient in the public sources is deck-level: HSReplay reports one Hearthstone deck at 48.8% at Diamond and 56.6% at Bronze ([HSReplay](https://articles.hsreplay.net/2020/04/07/rank-rework-faq/)). The design literature says skill-intensive cards are the high-ceiling, multi-step ones, but quantifies none. A paired-copy price (decklist against a copy with the card turned into a basic Energy, same AI pilot on both sides) measures a card's worth at that pilot's skill and no higher. The project's PlusPower prices already move with pilot strength: −0.054 with the greedy pilot, then about zero with search. They stay at or below zero even at the strongest pilot tried, so a stronger pilot is a plausible but unproven way to recover the 2-4 copies humans play. The earlier oracle-timing test cannot settle this, because it gave the list side perfect PlusPower timing at every pilot strength. The decisive experiment is a pilot-strength ladder with the oracle removed.

## What the sources say about skill and card worth

Public analytics mostly slice win rate by skill tier at the level of decks, not cards. HSReplay's rank FAQ warns that "All Ranks" data is polluted by low-rank play, and shows the Bronze-to-Diamond gap above; it gives no per-card breakdown ([HSReplay](https://articles.hsreplay.net/2020/04/07/rank-rework-faq/)) [read]. For Magic, 17Lands' "games in hand" win rate counts a game whenever the card was in hand, cast or not, and its defender concedes that it favors cards in controlling decks because long games show more cards ([MTG Arena Zone](https://mtgazone.com/17lands-in-defense-of-the-data/)) [read]. The same author says player skill is where a reader should override the data, because draft pods mix strengths. Both metrics are correlational and weight a card by the skill of whoever played it.

The qualitative framework points the same way. StarCityGames separates a card's floor ("what it does with no help from other cards") from its ceiling, and says high-ceiling cards give a deck "another gear when both players have a strong draw" ([StarCityGames](https://articles.starcitygames.com/articles/a-three-pronged-approach-to-card-evaluations/)) [read]. A Blizzard forum thread (a forum post, not an official source) defines skill cap as how far piloting skill moves a deck's win rate, and ties it to decision complexity and the number of actions ([Blizzard forum](https://us.forums.blizzard.com/en/hearthstone/t/question-understanding-skill-cap/82841)) [read]. PlusPower, Gust of Wind, Item Finder and Scoop Up fit that description: each is worth little when played on autopilot and a lot when played on the right turn against the right target.

Several items should be read as unverified. A search snippet reported top-tier 17Lands players at 60.7% against 57.1% for the middle tier, with a card-level tier gap near 10 points; the source article was never opened [snippet]. 17Lands' user-group filter on its card ratings was also seen only in a snippet, and its card tables were not retrieved. Retro-format claims about Energy Removal being "deemed too powerful" or "kind of slow" came from snippets of pages that returned 403 [snippet]. The conclusion that skill matters most for flexible, multi-step cards is reasoning from the framework, not a measurement. Hearthstone and Magic papers on deckbuilding and card valuation (Q-DeckRec, a Hearthstone price-versus-value thesis) were found only as abstracts or snippets, and none swaps a card for a basic resource and replays ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/1806.09771)) [read: abstract only]. LOCM and IEEE CoG deckbuilding work was not searched.

## Why a paired price measures worth at one skill level

The paired price is the win-rate difference between a list and the same list with the card replaced by an Energy, both piloted by one policy. That is a clean counterfactual with three built-in dependences, all inference from the method rather than findings from the card-game literature. First, it is conditional on the pilot: a policy that cannot time a card prices it low, and a low price is then what a weak pilot should produce, not evidence against the human lists. Second, it depends on the replacement: if the deck already carries many Energy, one more adds little, so almost any useful card looks worth about one Energy. Third, it depends on the field: it is one point in the space of (other 59 cards) times (opponent decks).

Card mechanics explain why timing is the crux for PlusPower. Under the project's rules reading (PlusPower applied after Weakness, from a March 2000 WotC chat via a secondary compilation), a +10 changes a result only when the target would be left at exactly 10 HP; under the alternative order it also flips Weakness targets at 10 to 20 HP left (derived from the earlier tempo report's table, with the card text from [pkmncards](https://pkmncards.com/card/electabuzz-base-set-bs-20/)). A pilot that plays PlusPower on every attack wastes most copies. Gust of Wind chooses which Pokémon is hit, so it creates the 10-HP and Weakness cases that PlusPower needs ([pkmncards](https://pkmncards.com/card/gust-of-wind-base-set-bs-93/)) [read]; the cards are worth more together than apart, which fits the human medians of about 2.75 PlusPower and 2.5 Gust.

## The project's prices rise with pilot strength, then stop at zero

All figures in this section are from the project log. PlusPower's paired price against the human field is **−0.054 (SE 0.011)** with the greedy pilot, **−0.003 (0.014)** with the pilot's own one-turn search on both seats, and **+0.011 (0.014)** with a Prize-scored search. Treating the estimates as independent (my arithmetic), greedy to own search is a rise of 0.051 with SE 0.018, about 2.9 SE, and greedy to Prize-scored search is 0.065, about 3.7 SE. Own search versus Prize-scored search is 0.014 on SE about 0.020, indistinguishable. So the price moves with pilot strength, but the step from a pilot that misuses the card to one that uses it sensibly is where the gain sits, and the best pilot tried still prices PlusPower near zero (+0.011, within one SE), not at the several-percent worth that 2-4 copies per list would imply. Only these three pilots exist, so this is a three-point ladder, not a trend.

Two reasons for caution about the earlier oracle test, which found PlusPower flat across three pilot strengths (0.51-0.52). The oracle timed PlusPower for the list side at every strength, so it removed exactly the skill variable the ladder is meant to vary. A flat result there says oracle timing is worth about one Energy at each strength; it says nothing about how much a pilot's own timing is worth. The result also fits the 10-HP arithmetic: a perfectly timed +10 on its own is a narrow card, and the full tempo package is not tested by a single-card swap.

The four-card result has a different problem. The four tempo cards together price at **−0.037**, which is **+0.065** above the sum of their single prices (so the singles sum to about −0.102, derived). A positive interaction is what the Gust-plus-PlusPower reasoning predicts, but it is confounded with diminishing returns: swapping four cards for four Energy adds four Energy at once, and the fifth to eighth extra Energy are worth less than the first. Interaction and saturation cannot be separated without holding Energy count fixed, for instance by replacing the four cards with four other non-Energy filler or by varying the baseline Energy count.

## Competing readings and what the evidence leaves open

Three readings fit the current data. In the skill reading, human players time these cards better than any project pilot, so the paired price understates their worth to humans, and the price will keep rising as the pilot improves. In the selection reading, the 2000 lists were built by players who chose cards they happened to draw and use well, so 2-4 copies reflect play skill and list-building habit, not marginal value in this engine. In the engine reading, some rule or parameter (damage order, Energy Removal density, the 25-minute clock, Scoop Up handling) differs from 2000, so the prices are low for reasons no pilot can fix. The project's own working rule is that consistent deviation from human lists means the pipeline is wrong, which favors checking the engine reading first. The ladder below tests the skill reading only; it cannot rule the engine reading out.

## Testable implications for the project

**1. A pilot-strength ladder of the paired price with no oracle.** Price each of PlusPower, Gust, Item Finder and Scoop Up singly, and the four together at fixed Energy count, with both seats using the same pilot at four or more strengths (greedy, own one-turn search, Prize-scored search, then a deeper or longer-lookahead search). Write the bands before the run: a price that rises monotonically and crosses a positive threshold (for example +0.03) at the strongest pilot, and replicates on a second seed, supports building a stronger reference player, because the missing worth is then pilot-limited. A price that stays within one SE of zero (as the 0.014 gap between the top two rungs already suggests) at every rung undercuts it, and shifts attention to the engine reading and the selection reading.

**2. Separate tempo interaction from Energy saturation.** Repeat the four-card swap with the added Energy count held fixed, by replacing the tempo cards with other non-Energy cards or by varying the baseline Energy count. If the +0.065 over the sum of singles survives, a stronger pilot should gain most on the package, and the reference player should be judged on PlusPower plus Gust jointly. If it shrinks toward zero, the interaction was mostly saturation, and a stronger pilot buys less than the ladder suggests.
