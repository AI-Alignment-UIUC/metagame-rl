# Branch rare decisions instead of reweighting them

Only two things multiply the training signal for a decision with a 0.02 value gap that a 30-minute run sees about 10,000 times: more visits to the decision state, and less noise per visit. Reweighting, prioritising or stratifying the samples you already have changes step sizes but adds no information. Self-imitation of won games mostly amplifies draw luck when the outcome is ±1. The strongest unbiased method in the notes is the TRPO "vine" estimator and its modern form, VinePPO. You snapshot each rare decision as it happens on-policy, play every candidate answer out to the end of the game with shared randomness, and use the averaged difference as the PPO advantage at that one state. The estimate stays unbiased for any number of rollouts, and in VinePPO's language-model tests it needed **2.8–9x fewer gradient steps** than PPO ([VinePPO](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.01679)). In this project, pairing alone is worth only about **2.5x**, because paired outcomes differ on about 20% of re-deals (a project measurement). Most of the gain therefore has to come from replacing PPO's whole-game credit with remainder-of-game comparisons and from branching each state several times. Restarts add visits with a bias that importance weights remove. Game-AI systems handled rare actions through structure rather than volume, and none has shown self-play learning what a tutor card is worth.

## Reweighting moves the step size, not the evidence

Telling two answers apart takes n ∝ σ²/Δ² samples, and a weight multiplies signal and noise together, so only a lower σ or more visits reduces n. The notes put a direct, unpaired comparison of two answers at about **20,000 visits per answer**, 40,000 in all (z = 2, σ = 1). PPO's 290,000 is about 7x more. My reading is that most of PPO's shortfall is credit noise: a choice made on turn 2 is scored by the whole game's outcome, routed through a critic. VinePPO makes the same point: critics with explained variance of **0.7–0.9** ranked candidate steps "barely" above chance ([VinePPO](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.01679)).

Prioritised sampling inside actor-critic methods has disappointed. LASER found that prioritised replay with full importance correction "performs no higher than with uniform replay" on DMLab-30 ([LASER](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1909.11583)). An ACER-style prioritised agent (ACPER) scored a **46.8%** median on Atari, against **96.1%** for plain A2C ([Oh et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1806.05635)).

Bias depends on what the weight is attached to. The decision type is a property of the state, so a weight w(s) keeps the true improvement direction at each state and only reweights the objective, a distortion PPO already carries by dropping γ^t ([Nota & Thomas](https://arxiv.org/abs/1906.07073)). A weight attached to the chosen action, such as upweighting whenever the pilot plays Computer Search, breaks baseline cancellation and is genuinely biased. Stratified minibatches that keep each epoch's expectation unchanged are bias-free; they steady the rare head's gradients but add no information.

The structural fixes also address dilution, not information. OpenAI Five gave Teleport its own head because its signal was "drowned out" in the shared targeting head ([Berner et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1912.06680)). AlphaStar updated its action heads independently to stop early trace cutting. In its ablation, V-trace alone reached **49%**, adding TD(λ) values **73%**, and adding UPGO **82%** ([AlphaStar](https://storage.googleapis.com/deepmind-media/research/alphastar/AlphaStar_unformatted.pdf)); the system as a whole also used human data. Reusing data from the last 4 policies cut samples by **15–77%** in GePPO ([GePPO](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2111.00072)), but reuse buys extra passes over the same data, not more information.

## Restarts pay off as paired branches, not as extra episodes

Restart theory explains why rare states learn slowly: the policy-gradient guarantee scales with how rarely the optimiser visits the states an optimal policy uses, and low visitation "effectively scales down the learning rate" there ([Kakade & Langford](https://hunch.net/~jl/projects/aoarl/Final.tex_back); [Agarwal et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1908.00261)). Any optimal policy is optimal under both the restart and the true start distribution, so restarts only cost accuracy through limited capacity.

The closest empirical recipe restarts PPO from the agent's own saved states, with 10% of transitions coming from restarts. On a sparse-reward task, the episodic-restart variant learned in **all 42 runs**, while the baseline failed in **80%** ([Tavakoli et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1811.11298)). The kept runs were filtered by early success, though. Backplay and reverse curricula grow outward from a demonstration or a goal state ([Backplay](https://arxiv.org/abs/1807.06919); [Florensa et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1707.05300)), which a card game lacks, and the pilot's problem is credit, not discovery. Credit is what TRPO's vine addresses: roll out several actions from the same state with common random numbers (CRN) to "greatly reduce the variance of the Q-value differences" ([TRPO](https://arxiv.org/abs/1502.05477)).

Restoring the full game state leaves Q and V unbiased at every state; only the state weighting of the loss changes ([Tavakoli et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1811.11298)). There are three ways to handle that. The vine approach branches only states that occurred on-policy and keeps the rollouts' later states out of the PPO batch, so it is exact ([VinePPO](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.01679)). A restart pool drawn from on-policy visits with a known weight w(s) can be corrected exactly by the ratio 1/w(s). Plain mixing at a fraction β shifts the objective by at most β/(1−β) of the value range, which is 0.22 at β = 0.1. That bound is the notes' own derivation, not a published result, and annealing β removes the shift by the end of training.

Hidden cards add one more constraint. In imperfect-information games a state's value depends on a posterior over hidden cards, and that posterior is conditioned on the opponent's policy ([ReBeL](https://arxiv.org/abs/2007.13544)). Re-dealing the opponent's hand uniformly is therefore biased. The opponent hand from the real game is an exact posterior sample, so keep it, along with every card a player has seen. Reshuffle only the cards neither player has seen. Search and recovery cards change the deck, so a key-based shuffle (an untested design from the notes) is needed to keep the branches correlated after the choice.

The project's numbers set the budget (estimates). If paired outcomes differ on a share q ≈ 0.2 of re-deals, the paired variance 4q = 0.8 is 2.5x below the unpaired 2.0, and about 40,000·q ≈ **8,000 pairs** resolve a 0.02 gap. If the remaining game averages half a game, one pair costs about one game. Ten thousand decisions at 0.4–1.7 per game means roughly 6,000–25,000 games per 30 minutes, so half the budget buys about 3,000–12,000 pairs. That is borderline. Branching should therefore favour states where the outcome is still open (win probability between 0.1 and 0.9, as Florensa et al. used), and states should be chosen by a generic rarity or TD-error rule rather than by card name.

## Self-imitation turns ±1 outcomes into luck amplifiers

Self-imitation learning (SIL) imitates past actions whose return R beat the critic's estimate V. It raised A2C's median on Atari from **96.1% to 138.7%**, but its justification assumes a deterministic environment ([Oh et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1806.05635)). With R ∈ {±1}, the SIL policy term collapses to "reinforce every win". On-policy, that is a rescaled policy gradient with about twice the variance and no gain in signal-to-noise. Replay and win-prioritisation then reward whatever fetch targets happened to coincide with good draws.

The evidence on luck is consistent. SAIL's average gain over DQN fell from **+231.6% to +101.4%** when sticky actions added randomness, and SAIL dropped prioritisation because it "emphasizes the impact of stochasticity" ([SAIL](https://arxiv.org/abs/2012.11989)). Cloning winning trajectories in a gambling task yields **−0.17** expected return "no matter how much data". Against a stochastic Connect Four opponent, a return-conditioned Decision Transformer scored 0.80, while ESPER, which averages out chance, scored **0.99** ([Paster et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.15967)).

The fix is to replace each single return with an average over re-deals, which a re-deal simulator provides exactly. A "paired SIL" that imitates the best answer only when its paired advantage clears z·SE does not appear in the literature, and it reduces to the vine estimator. A gentler target is Expert Iteration's soft, visit-weighted target, which is "cost-sensitive" when options are nearly tied ([ExIt](https://arxiv.org/abs/1705.08439)). Hindsight baselines that see hidden cards stay unbiased if that information is independent of the action ([CCA](https://arxiv.org/abs/2011.09464)), but need checking against the oracle rule.

## Game AI learned rare actions through structure, not volume

OpenAI Five scripted item purchasing rather than learning it, and randomised Roshan's health because agents had learned "never to approach this creature". Its Earthshaker agent misplayed Fissure, so the drafting preferences valued that hero low ([Berner et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1912.06680)). That is the pattern this project would see if its pilot misplays Computer Search and so prices it below a basic Energy.

Under prioritised fictitious self-play alone, TStarBot-X's main agent converged to "Zergling Rush or Roach Push" within days ([TStarBot-X](https://arxiv.org/abs/2011.13729)), much like this project's Energy-heavy minimum. DouZero scores each of **27,472** actions from card-content features, so rarely played bombs still get informed values ([DouZero](https://arxiv.org/abs/2106.06135)).

Several systems keep every action in play without leaking that exploration into the learned policy. KataGo's forced playouts, combined with pruning the forced visits out of the policy target, were worth about **1.25x** in training efficiency ([KataGo](https://arxiv.org/abs/1902.10565)). Pluribus floored regret so pruned actions could return ([Pluribus](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.aay2400)), and ReBeL had one player explore with ε = 0.25 ([ReBeL](https://arxiv.org/abs/2007.13544)).

No paper measures self-play learning the value of tutor or search cards. Stronger self-play can also drift away from human conventions. DouZero's agreement with human moves rose for five days and then fell ([DouZero](https://arxiv.org/abs/2106.06135)), and DORA's equilibrium "differs radically" from that of human-data agents ([DORA](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.02924)).

## Four arms ranked for the fixed-deck bench

Every arm runs for 30 minutes against a plain-PPO control at matched wall-clock time. Each reports per-card Trainer prices against a basic Energy (the ground truth), head-to-head win rate against the control, and the measured standard error per decision.

| Rank | Arm (unbiased form) | Extra things to measure | Main risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **CRN vine advantages**: branch each rare decision that occurs on-policy, play out all candidates (K re-deals, shared seeds), and use the branch advantage at that state only. Variant: an ExIt-style soft target over the branch values. | Measured q, the share of compute spent on branches, pairs per decision type | Compute: the ~8,000 pairs needed may not fit in 30 minutes; the encoder may not separate the answers |
| 2 | **Arm 1 plus a restart pool**: 10% of episodes start from saved rare-decision states from the last few iterations, weighted by 1/w(s) | Visits per decision type, drift in head-to-head strength | Stale hidden-card posteriors; a biased objective if the weights are dropped |
| 3 | **One-sided forced Trainer exploration**: one seat sometimes plays legal Trainers, and those steps are removed from the policy target but still train the value | How often Trainers are played, value at states after a search | Off-policy steps leaking into the policy; only adds visits |
| 4 | **Control: separate sub-decision head with its own clipped ratio, plus stratified minibatches** | The same metrics; a null result is expected | No information gain, so a negative result is uninformative about arms 1–3 |

## Conclusion

The project's own results point to where the bottleneck is. A ridge readout fitted to paired returns matched the capacity of one fitted to oracle answers, yet it picked no better answers than the pilot. My reading is that extra signal has to train the encoder through the policy loss, which arm 1 does, rather than feed a side head that reads frozen option vectors. The weaker alternative is that the true gaps at this skill level are smaller than 0.02. Since q ≈ 0.2 makes CRN a modest multiplier, the deciding 5-minute check is whether branch advantages give a per-decision standard error far below PPO's at equal compute. If they do not, the only lever left in this literature is raw extra visits from restarts, each still carrying single-outcome noise. Plain PPO already got stronger without raising Trainer prices, so head-to-head gains alone cannot justify keeping an arm.
