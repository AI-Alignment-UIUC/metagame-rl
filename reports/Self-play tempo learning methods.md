# Evidence for tempo is thin; three tests rank first

The literature we could read does not document a self-play agent that learned to stall and was then cured by a named method, so no proposal below rests on strong evidence. The nearest documented lever is a value for draws and clock outcomes that sits below a win, as in KataGo and Leela Chess Zero, and the nearest documented structural fix is population training, as in AlphaStar. Per-step time costs have only snippet-level support. We rank three proposals for the Pokémon TCG pilot: (1) a speed-dependent win payout that leaves losses untouched, (2) lowering the clock-outcome values already in the 2000 round rule, and (3) a gameplay league of frozen self-trained past snapshots. All three are cheap; the first two are one-line reward edits, and the third reuses existing checkpoints. The source notes were gathered in roughly 12 tool calls; AlphaZero, KataGo, AlphaStar (Nature) and OpenAI Five full texts were unreadable, and most cited papers were seen only as an abstract or search snippet. Treat every ranking below as a prior to be tested, not a finding.

## What the sources show, and what they do not

The AlphaStar blog, the one primary page the researchers fully read, says agents trained in a league of frozen older competitors, each with its own objective, and that early "risky strategies were discarded as training progressed" ([DeepMind](https://deepmind.google/discover/blog/alphastar-mastering-the-real-time-strategy-game-starcraft-ii/)). That is the opposite direction from our problem: league pressure there removed rushes rather than creating them. The same page says the final agent comes from the Nash mixture of league members and must "not forget how to defeat earlier ones." Nothing in it shows that exploiters punish slow play specifically; the notes say the main-exploiter mechanism could not be verified.

The documented pathologies in the other sources are cycling or collapse, not stalling for a clock. DeepNash's abstract claims its R-NaD method converges to an approximate Nash equilibrium instead of cycling around it, without search ([arXiv 2206.15378](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.15378)); PSRO trains best responses to mixtures of past policies ([arXiv 1711.00832](https://arxiv.org/abs/1711.00832)). Neither abstract mentions passivity. A snippet-level result (not opened) reports a Q-learning agent in Kuhn poker collapsing onto a deterministic exploitative strategy within a few episodes ([arXiv 2605.16315](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.16315)). Our inference, not a sourced claim: a pilot that runs out the clock is better described as optimizing the reward it was given, where a clock outcome pays 2/3 or 1/3, than as a self-play pathology. That points to payout design first and population design second.

For card games, the notes found **no verified paper in which tempo or aggression emerges from pure self-play with no human data or heuristics**. Hearthstone and LOCM work found was supervised, heuristic or search-based; one 2021 LOCM winner reportedly trained draft networks by self-play, but only via a secondary summary ([arXiv 2404.16689](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.16689)). Xi et al. (arXiv 2303.04096, optimistic smooth fictitious play on LOCM) is the first paper worth opening and was not read. The state of the art there is called "highly exploitable" by those authors, with results labeled preliminary, so card-game self-play is not a settled success story.

## Proposal 1: pay wins by speed, leave losses alone

The notes propose a fixed cost per own turn on every trajectory. Applied to losses, that makes a fast loss cheaper than a slow one, which is the same flaw as the failed discount (it rewards ending a losing game early or stalling differently). Our modification, which is an inference and not in the notes: reduce only the payout of a win by c per own turn it took, for example win = 1 - c*(turn - 6), floored above the 2/3 clock-win value, and leave losses and draws as the round rule pays them. The mechanism is that a Prize win on turn 10 beats one on turn 25 without making any loss cheaper, so the gradient points at tempo while the ordering win > clock win > draw > loss is preserved. This differs from the tried discount because the penalty never touches the loss branch.

Evidence is **weak**. The supporting literature is two search snippets: per-step penalties speed learning in tabular Q-learning ([arXiv 2205.15400](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2205.15400)), and a 2024 result that a fixed -1 per step beats dense reward (found in search results, not opened, no link recorded). Both are small-domain results and do not address stalling. The notes also record only a general warning, from search results and no primary paper, that time penalties can be dodged by a draw or resignation, which is why the loss branch must stay fixed and the speed bonus must never exceed the gap to the clock-win value.

Falsifier: mean own turns to six Prizes does not fall (target well below the current 19-32, toward the scripted line's roughly 9) on a second seed, or win rate against a fixed reference falls, or the draw rate rises. Run the usual 30-minute stage with a stop rule.

## Proposal 2: lower the clock outcomes, not the win

The 2000 rule already pays a time-win 2/3 and a draw 1/3, so a clock result is already valued below a Prize win; the notes' "draw-equivalent value" idea is therefore partly built in. What remains untested is how far apart the payouts sit. KataGo exposes a draw-equivalent value as a parameter (`drawEquivalentWinsForWhite`, whose default and the 0.75 example come only from a forum post by the developer, [lightvector](https://lifein19x19.com/viewtopic.php?p=251740), secondary and not fully read). Leela's contempt setting is described as discouraging early draws against weaker opponents ([Chess Programming Wiki](https://chessprogramming.org/Contempt_Factor), snippet), and a TalkChess report of a 420-Elo cap on it is opinion-level. The mechanism: if a pilot is getting 2/3 for a safe time-win, shrinking that to about 0.4 (draw 0.1) makes a risky race for six Prizes worth more than a clock grind. This is training-only reward; evaluation keeps the real 2/3 and 1/3.

Evidence is **thin and secondary**; it shows only that engine authors treat the draw value as a tunable bias. It is the cheapest test (two constants) and is also a direct probe of whether the clock payout explains the stalling. Falsifier: the share of games ending on the turn limit and the 41-45% of turns with no payable attack (a figure from the project's own notes) do not move, or win rate against the fixed reference drops with no tempo gain. Because it shares an axis with Proposal 1, test the two separately and then together, not nested.

## Proposal 3: a gameplay league of the pilot's own frozen past selves

Train the pilot against a mixture of its own frozen earlier checkpoints plus the current self, in the AlphaStar and PSRO style, with no scripted opponent. The mechanism is that a stalling policy is only safe against opponents that also stall; an earlier or differently-seeded snapshot that happens to race will punish it, and the league keeps it from forgetting. This differs from the already-tried exploiters and challengers, which sat in the drafting league. Here the league is over game-play policies.

Evidence is **the weakest of the three for our goal**: AlphaStar's own account shows leagues removing risky rushes, so it may entrench slow play rather than cure it ([DeepMind](https://deepmind.google/discover/blog/alphastar-mastering-the-real-time-strategy-game-starcraft-ii/)). Its value is as a robustness check on Proposals 1 and 2 rather than a standalone fix, and it costs more (several concurrent checkpoints and a matchmaking rule). Falsifier: against the league mix, turns to six Prizes stay at 19-32 after a 30-minute stage, or the strongest snapshot in the pool is not faster than the current agent, which would show no fast line exists in the population to be rewarded.

## Conclusion

The research did not find a proven recipe, so the ranking is by cost and by mechanism fit: the two reward-shape edits come first because they test the one concrete suspect, the clock payouts, in under an hour each, and a null on both would argue that the stall is a search or credit-assignment problem rather than a payout problem. That would shift the next step toward Evan's architecture and memory route, not toward more reward tuning.

The most useful next reading is Xi et al. on LOCM and the AlphaStar and AlphaZero full texts, because no source here shows a card-game agent acquiring tempo from self-play alone. Until then, every number in this report except the pilot's own turn counts is a literature prior from abstracts, snippets or forum posts.
