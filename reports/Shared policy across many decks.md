# Shared pilots lose skill, and cheap fixes come first

The size of the skill loss for one pilot across about 99 decks is unmeasured. No card-game or board-game result comparing a shared policy with per-deck specialists was found, and no matched single-task versus shared numbers at equal experience budget were extracted from full-text sources. The nearest measured gap is on Meta-World, where multi-task PPO reached only 30.5% (MT10) and 35.4% (MT50) average success and the authors say the algorithms "struggle to learn with multiple tasks at the same time, even with as few as ten distinct training tasks" ([Meta-World](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1910.10897), full text; [abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/1910.10897), abstract). Applying that to a two-player card game is an assumption. Of the recovery methods, the best-supported one is cheap: per-task return normalization (PopArt, snippet only). Gradient-surgery optimizers had no edge over a plain summed loss in the one study read in full. Specialist-plus-shared designs (Distral, kickstarting) cost roughly one policy per deck, and the evidence for them covers 4-8 tasks. Every method below fits the human-free rule, provided any specialist is trained only on decks the loop drafted itself. Grades follow the notes: full text, abstract, snippet, memory.

## The gap is real on Meta-World but unmeasured for decks

Meta-World Table 1 (10 seeds, full text) gives MT10/MT50 success of 30.5%/35.4% for multi-task PPO, 31.3%/21.0% for TRPO, 20.9%/11.8% for task embeddings, and 68.3%/38.5% for multi-task SAC; the evidence is that the gap widens with task count ([Meta-World](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1910.10897), full text). Single-task rates are not tabulated in that paper. Appendix B says only that SAC can learn all 50 tasks "to some degree" and PPO "a large majority" (3 seeds, no numbers; full text). So even the Meta-World gap is "large and task-count dependent" rather than a specific size. Atari points the other way: one network on 8 games reached near-expert on 7 within about 20 epochs ([Actor-Mimic review](https://liner.com/review/actormimic-deep-multitask-and-transfer-reinforcement-learning), snippet). Meta-World's gap comes from continuous control with different reward scales, so whether a shared pilot loses skill on Trainer play is an empirical question for us. The note's one Hearthstone lead ([AAMAS 2025](https://aamas.csc.liv.ac.uk/Proceedings/aamas2025/pdfs/p2795.pdf), snippet, no numbers read) and a 2026 arXiv paper ([unopened](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.28863)) give nothing usable.

## Training on many decks helps, but 99 is far from unlimited

On CoinRun, test score rose with the number of training levels: **66.58 at 100 levels, 84.84 at 1000, 95.87 at 4000, 98.29 unlimited** ([Cobbe 2019](https://arxiv.org/html/1812.02341v3), full text, Table 1, 5 seeds). With the Nature-CNN, train score fell from 99.45 to 90.87 as levels grew, so a small fixed pool looks better in training than it generalizes. The paper reports "substantial overfitting" below 4,000 levels. This supports many varied decks over few fixed ones, and it warns that a train-test gap on unseen decks is likely at 99. The evidence is about levels in one game, not decks, so the mapping is an assumption. Procgen itself ([Cobbe 2020](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1912.01588), snippet) adds only that the default is 500 training levels.

## Conditioned and modular pilots beat plain sharing only in the authors' tables

On Meta-World MT50 with random goals, PaCo scored 57.3 versus CARE 50.8, multi-task SAC 49.3 and SAC+FiLM 36.5; on MT10, PaCo scored 85.4 versus CARE 76.0 ([PaCo supplement](https://papers.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2022/file/86b8ad667206fb9a52ae575fbf1cd6be-Supplemental-Conference.pdf), snippet). The authors re-ran baselines on Meta-World-V2 without the reward normalizer, and with fixed goals plain multi-task SAC can be competitive. A second review gives CARE MT10 0.84 against 0.49 for multi-task SAC ([liner.com](https://liner.com/review/multitask-reinforcement-learning-with-contextbased-representations), snippet, unchecked against the paper), while PTSL could not reproduce CARE's results even with CARE's code ([PTSL](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2309.08776), snippet). Soft modularization reports gains mostly on harder goal-conditioned MT50 ([abstract/snippet](https://papers.nips.cc/paper/2020/hash/32cfdce9631d8c7906e8e9d6e68b514b-Abstract.html); numeric table not read). Our pilot already conditions on the deck through cross-attention, which is the same family, so the open question is whether a routed or parameter-composed deck module beats it. No card-game head-to-head exists, and FiLM and hypernetworks appear only as baselines. Because the deck is observed by the pilot, these designs need no human card knowledge.

## Gradient surgery adds little, return normalization is the one measured win

Kurin et al. find a plain summed loss with standard regularization on par with specialized multi-task optimizers on MT10, with no optimizer significantly better on MT50; unitary scalarization ran about 15 hours per MT50 run versus more than a week for PCGrad, MGDA and GradDrop ([Kurin 2022](https://arxiv.org/html/2201.04122), full text, text only; RL results are in figures). Xin et al. report the same on language and vision ([abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2209.11379); their RL results were not read). A PCGrad re-run gave MT10 fixed-goal 81.8 ± 5.2 versus multi-task SAC 84.5 ± 12.4, in a different setup (PaCo supplement, snippet). The one method with clear measured value is PopArt value normalization, reported at **110% versus 59.7%** median human-normalized score over 57 Atari games ([PopArt](https://research.google/pubs/multi-task-deep-reinforcement-learning-with-popart/), snippet; the 110% came from a truncated excerpt and needs checking). No PopArt result exists for two-player or card settings. Reweighting decks by inverse frequency has no direct evidence here; its effect on deck-selection bias is unmeasured. CAGrad and Rusu's policy distillation were not read (memory only).

## Specialists help, but cost one policy per deck

Distral keeps a shared policy and per-task workers regularized to it by KL. It learned faster and finished better than multi-task A3C on maze and navigation tasks, but on laser-tag the best baseline was single-task A3C. Budgets were 4x10^8 steps per task on 4-8 tasks, with results shown qualitatively in figures ([Distral](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1707.04175), full text; [abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/1707.04175)). Compute and memory scale with task count, so 99 decks means 99 specialists, and no cost at matched total steps was readable. Kickstarting matched from-scratch performance in about 1/10 of the steps and finished 42% higher on DMLab-30 ([Schmitt 2018](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1803.03835), abstract as quoted in snippet). A reader's notes warn the student may ignore an incompetent teacher ([notes](https://yobibyte.github.io/files/paper_notes/Kickstarting_Deep_Reinforcement_Learning_Simon_Schmitt__Jonathan_J__Hudson__Augustin_Zidek_et_al___2018.pdf), snippet). That is exactly the risk when the shared pilot plays Trainers weakly, since a specialist cloned from it inherits the weakness. Actor-Mimic's multitask-to-transfer pipeline can take over 3 weeks on a fast GPU per the repo notes ([repo](https://github.com/eparisotto/ActorMimic), snippet).

## Conclusion

The notes support one actionable conclusion: before paying for any recovery method, measure the specialist-versus-shared gap on a few self-drafted decks, because every number in this literature comes from continuous control, Atari or level-based platformers. If the gap shows up mostly in Trainer play, the kickstarting caveat means distilling from the shared pilot will not fix it, which favors normalization and conditioning changes over specialists.

Evidence is thin where it matters. All reported gains from CARE, PaCo and PopArt are snippet-grade, CARE has a reproducibility challenge, and the full-text sources (Meta-World, Distral, Kurin, Cobbe) do not include a two-player card game. The searches were English-only, and topics skipped by project list include co-adaptation, prioritized level replay, and exploration.
