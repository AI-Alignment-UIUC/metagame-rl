# Teach the pilot to hold PlusPower

The best-supported fix is to change what a "hold" branch is compared against, not to add a cleverer credit estimator. The pilot's comparator reads about +0.09 in conversion states and about 0 elsewhere. That is what you would expect if the hold branch is rolled out by the same always-play policy: the held card gets burned at the next ordinary attack, so holding and playing tie at non-conversion rows, and nothing in the gradient pushes toward holding. This is my inference from your measurements, not a tested result, and the value function has the matching blind spot, since it only ever sees returns from a policy that never holds. I found **no published study of learning when to hold a consumable**; the nearest game-AI work (OpenAI Five, AlphaStar, Hearthstone MCTS, NetHack) scripted or ignored the question. The recommended arms are therefore built from general tools. In order: a hold-aware continuation policy for the branch comparator (A), forced-hold exploration with the exploration kept out of the policy target (B), full-game CRN vine advantages on the play-or-hold decision (C), auxiliary value heads (D), and a return-decomposition probe (E). Variance-reduction methods (all-action gradients, hindsight baselines) cannot fix a signal that is zero in expectation, so they are add-ons at best. Two designs add hand-built or game-specific terms and are flagged below: the "option-value shadow price" and any continuation gated by a simulator check of "does +10 convert a knockout".

## Why a zero-signal comparator keeps the policy at "always play"

With two candidates, the all-action gradient at a row is p(play)·A(play) + p(hold)·A(hold). If both advantages are about 0 at non-conversion rows, the gradient there is 0, and the positive +0.09 rows drive the shared parameters toward play. That is the same bias with the sampling noise removed (inference from the notes' algebra). Mean Actor-Critic, Expected Policy Gradients and the numerical-integration variants all cut variance while keeping the same expected gradient, so they inherit the blind spot ([Mean Actor-Critic](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1709.00503v2); [Expected Policy Gradients](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AAAI/article/view/11607); [Petit et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1910.09093)). The value function has the same loop: V(hold) exceeds V(play) only if V has seen returns where a held card paid off, and the on-policy data barely contain holds. That is why a longer lambda or n-step target alone is not enough; it spreads a later payoff back only if the payoff occurs in the data (inference). Your measurements fit this reading: the card's price stayed at 0.455 against 0.442 while oracle timing reaches 0.52, so the missing 0.065 is exactly the part that requires holds to be visited and valued.

The consequence for a 30-minute arm is that the first thing to fix is data and the comparison baseline, not the estimator. A hold branch needs a continuation policy that would actually use the card well, or an exploration scheme that makes held-card futures appear in the batch. Both are standard practice in search-improved training (the continuation is a one-step improvement of the current policy), but I did not retrieve a source for the exact recipe, so treat it as design reasoning.

## Branching and exploration are the tools that reach the missing data

The closest strong method is the TRPO "vine" estimator and its modern form: snapshot a decision, play each candidate out with common random numbers, and use the averaged difference as the advantage ([TRPO](https://arxiv.org/abs/1502.05477); [VinePPO](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.01679)). VinePPO reported **2.8 to 9 times fewer gradient steps** than PPO in language-model tests, and found that critics with explained variance of 0.7 to 0.9 ranked candidate steps barely above chance, which is the failure you describe at the card-timing level. In your simulator pairing is worth only about 2.5x (paired outcomes differ on about 20% of re-deals, a project measurement from the earlier report on rare decisions), so most of the benefit must come from remainder-of-game credit instead of whole-game credit routed through the critic. Hidden information limits this: re-deal only cards neither player has seen, and keep the real opponent hand, which is an exact posterior sample ([ReBeL](https://arxiv.org/abs/2007.13544)).

For exploration, KataGo's forced playouts, with the forced visits pruned from the policy target, bought about **1.25x** training efficiency, and Pluribus floored regret so pruned actions could return ([KataGo](https://arxiv.org/abs/1902.10565); [Pluribus](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.aay2400)). The analogue here is an epsilon-hold override at attack-available rows, with the action probability logged, steps removed from the policy target, and the value head still trained on them. Plain PPO restarts from saved states help sparse tasks (42 of 42 runs learned against an 80% baseline failure rate), but those runs were filtered by early success and the task was a discovery problem rather than a credit problem ([Tavakoli et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1811.11298)).

Credit-redistribution methods are the weakest fit. RUDDER regresses the episode return and redistributes reward by contribution analysis; it is exponentially faster on artificial delayed-reward tasks by the authors' account, but it is optimal only with a perfect return predictor, and its effect on Atari was mixed ([RUDDER](https://arxiv.org/abs/1806.07857); [reviews](https://papers.nips.cc/paper_files/paper/2019/file/16105fb9cc614fc29e1bda00dab60d41-Reviews.html)). A win or loss over dozens of turns is a weak target for an LSTM fitted in 30 minutes, and the redistributed reward would land on the later knockout step, not necessarily on the hold. Align-RUDDER needs demonstrations and is excluded ([Align-RUDDER](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v162/patil22a.html)). Hindsight Credit Assignment and Counterfactual Credit Assignment cut variance by conditioning on future information ([HCA](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper/2019/hash/195f15384c2a79cedf293e4a847ce85c-Abstract.html); [CCA](https://arxiv.org/abs/2011.09464)). CCA needs that information to be independent of the action, and holding changes whether PlusPower is still available later, so a baseline conditioned on "a knockout chance is coming" would be biased here. Synthetic Returns, which learns which earlier states predict a distant reward, is the closest in spirit and reported a 25x speedup on Atari Skiing ([Synthetic Returns](https://arxiv.org/abs/2102.12425)), but it has no consumable-timing test. Auxiliary tasks (UNREAL) raised Labyrinth performance from 54% to 87% of expert human ([UNREAL](https://alphaxiv.org/abs/1611.05397)). That shows general representation gains, not a fix for hold valuation.

## Five 30-minute arms for the fixed-deck bench

All arms run against a plain-PPO control at matched wall-clock, with bands for the 0.442 to 0.455 price range written before the run and a second seed required before any effect is claimed. Every arm should report four things: PlusPower price against basic Energy (ground truth, oracle ceiling 0.52), the play rate at non-conversion rows, the share of PlusPower plays that fall in conversion states, and head-to-head win rate against the control.

| Arm | Change | Measure beyond the four | Main risk | Human-prior flag |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A. Hold-aware branch continuation | Turn-end comparator over two own turns, 4 to 8 CRN copies. The continuation plays PlusPower only when the agent's own one-turn comparator is positive; iterate as the policy improves | Branch hold-minus-play gap at non-conversion rows (should move from 0 to above 0 where a conversion follows); noise per row | A noisy comparator near 0 makes the continuation hold forever or never; branch cost about 2x | Clean if gated by the agent's own comparator. **Flag:** gating by a simulator check that +10 converts a knockout is an oracle shortcut; use only as an upper-bound diagnostic |
| B. Forced-hold exploration | Epsilon override to hold at attack-available rows, probability logged, steps removed from the policy target, kept in value training | Fraction of games with a hold followed by a conversion; value of held states | Off-policy leakage into the policy; adds visits only | Clean (generic exploration) |
| C. Full-game CRN vine advantage on play-or-hold | Snapshot each attack-available row, play both candidates to game end, K=8 re-deals, advantage at that state only | Standard error per decision against PPO's at equal compute; pairs per decision | About 8,000 pairs may not fit in 30 minutes; the continuation still plays the card at the first chance, so hold still scores near zero unless combined with A | Clean |
| D. Auxiliary value heads | Heads on the value trunk predicting future prizes taken and future PlusPower plays within k turns, targets from real trajectories | Value gap between holding and not holding in conversion-soon states; probe accuracy | V still reads these features from policy data, so V(hold) may not rise without B | Mild **flag:** a head defined as "knockout-convertible attack turns" is hand-defined; the generic version predicts prizes taken and card-play events |
| E. Return decomposition on branch differences | Fit a small sequence model to branch value differences (not wins) and redistribute onto earlier steps; or a simplified synthetic-returns regression of later prize gain on hand contents | Does contribution analysis mark the held state; predictor error | Highest cost, noisy target, uncertain benefit | Clean |

The ranking I recommend is A plus B together first, because they address the missing data and the zero baseline directly, then C as the unbiased check, then D, then E. All-action or expected policy gradients are worth switching on inside A only as a free variance reduction, since alone they provide no push.

## What to avoid or label

The "option-value shadow price" idea in the notes, estimating the extra value of a held card as P(knockout turn arrives) times the roughly 0.09 gain and charging it when PlusPower is played at non-knockout rows, is a **hand-built shaping term** even though the two numbers come from your own logs. It also bakes in the card-specific structure the project forbids. Use it at most as a labeled diagnostic that bounds how much a correct price could add, never as a training signal. Likewise, any feature or target that names knockout margin, or a rule that holds PlusPower unless a simulator one-ply check says conversion, is game-specific and should not enter training. The CCA-style baseline conditioned on a coming knockout opportunity is not a shaping term but carries the bias noted above, so run it only with an independence check.

## Conclusion

The failure looks less like a credit-assignment problem than a baseline problem: the evaluator and the continuation both treat a held card as a card that will be wasted, so a better estimator of the same quantity returns the same near-zero. That makes the cheapest informative experiment an A-versus-B comparison, and it gives a sharp prediction. If A raises the hold-minus-play gap at non-conversion rows but the policy still plays the card, the problem is the policy update or encoder; if A shows no gap, the hold futures in the branch are still too rare, and B or C is the next step.

Evidence limits: I found no consumable-timing study in Hearthstone, Magic, poker, Dota or StarCraft, I read abstracts and summaries rather than full texts for most cited methods, and none of these arms has been run on your simulator, so every recommendation above is an untested inference.
