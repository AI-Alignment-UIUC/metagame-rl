# Fix the update rule, then the draft statistics

The literature does not hand this project a representation fix, and it points away from the usual exploration fixes. Ian Davis's Dominion series uses a tiny state and gained mostly from action masking and thermometer-style counts, which tells us little about hidden zones or situational Trainers. The better-supported lead is on the learning side. For a softmax policy, a rare action's gradient scales with its own probability, so a pilot that already gives PlusPower about zero probability gets almost no push back up, and an entropy bonus of 0.01 scales the same way. That points at the update geometry (exponentiated-advantage or covariance-throttled updates), not at more exploration noise or a new input feature. For drafting, the arithmetic says single 10-game moves cannot resolve +0.005 to +0.02 effects, so the 16% pass rate means most accepted moves are noise. The fix is to pool evidence across decks and re-test winners. Six experiments follow, ranked by evidence per unit cost. Most evidence below is snippet-level or from memory, and each claim says so.

## Dominion transfers masking and counts, not hidden-zone encoding

Davis's tabular Dominion agent saw only turn, score difference and Provinces left. It had no hand, deck or discard, and visited 763 of 1,620 possible states ([part 1](https://ianwdavis.com/dominion.html)). Part 2 reports the gains: a "progressively turned-on binary flag" (thermometer) encoding of counts took win rate from under 20% to about 30%, then about 40% with further count features. Setting illegal-action logits to -inf was called "the biggest breakthrough" ([part 2](https://ianwdavis.com/dominion2.html)). Things that did not help included doubling or halving the learning rate, a -0.5 "suicide" penalty (it cut suicides but not losses), and ReLU. Part 3's only deck-aware mechanism is a hand-coded Cellar rule that compares each hand card with the expected value of a card drawn from the known deck contents ([part 3](https://ianwdavis.com/dominion3.html)). That is the one idea close to draw-card valuation, and it is hand-written. These pages were read through a model-written summary, and part 3 gave no game counts or feature list for its networks, so none of it is verified verbatim.

Other card-game AIs add little for play. Hidden-hand work uses belief predictors (Gin Rummy, Big 2) and only snippets were seen ([Gin Rummy](https://github.com/rxng8/GinRummyAlgorithm), [Big 2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.28863)). The Axie Infinity agent encodes each action as a structured tuple, learns an action embedding, and scores state and action together. It won 49.86% against 44.77% for the DouZero baseline over 174k games, but with one game, no seeds and no confidence interval ([Axie](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2206.12700)). The project's pilot already does "options scored against the state", so Axie confirms the design rather than adding to it.

The most useful warning comes from Slay the Spire. One developer found that adding card-ID embeddings raised offline accuracy (55.8% vs 53.5%) but dropped the agent from 1st to 4th of 4 in play over 50 seeds, and concluded that the embeddings "learned human pick biases" ([write-up](https://dev.to/doronsinger/part-3-good-models-finish-last-2n6a)). This is one author with no interval, so treat it as a hypothesis. It bears on the project because the identity embeddings are learned from decks, and the project log shows draw-card skill appears only after training on coherent decks. Attribute and text features generalise to unseen cards in MTG drafting (33.57% vs 23.79% for random vectors on unseen sets), but that metric is agreement with human picks, not win rate ([arXiv 2407.05879](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.05879v1)).

Pointing options at encoder latents, and the entity and pointer designs of ygo-agent, AlphaStar and OpenAI Five, are covered in the earlier unified-token report and are not repeated here. This report treats them as the baseline architecture.

## Entropy and forced noise cannot lift a near-zero action

The key mechanism is a derivation, not a measurement, and it comes from memory. For a softmax policy, the expected policy-gradient push on action i's logit is about pi(i) times (Q(i) minus V). An action at 0.4% probability gets 0.4% of the push it would get at certainty. The entropy bonus has the same pi(i) scaling. The project log is consistent with this: the critic credits PlusPower, the policy plays it near 0%, and the policy's own full-width search plays it at 83%. Check the identity against Agarwal et al. 2020 and Mei et al. 2020 on softmax policy-gradient convergence before citing it. Both are known to show slow convergence from badly initialised softmax policies, but neither was opened here.

Two published fixes act on this geometry. Mirror-descent policy optimisation uses an update of the form pi_new proportional to pi_old^(1-eta) times exp(eta Q), which has no pi(i) factor, so a high-value rare action grows exponentially with steps instead of linearly in a tiny probability ([Tomar et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.09814), snippet; the connection to the PlusPower case is my inference). Clip-Cov and KL-Cov throttle updates on tokens with high covariance between log-probability and advantage, which keeps entropy up in LLM reasoning RL ([Cui et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.22617), snippet, token-level, not card games). The pilot's dominant plays would be the high-covariance ones, so throttling them could free mass for rare plays. Neither method has card-game evidence.

The other options are weaker for this problem. Randomized value heads act on the critic's own disagreement ([Osband et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1703.07608), [prior functions](https://arxiv.org/abs/1806.03335), snippets), which targets the right gap but needs importance correction. NGU-style novelty targets states, not actions ([Badia et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2002.06038)). Gumbel top-k sampling draws from the policy's logits, so it cannot sample a near-zero action without a mixed proposal, and that mixture is close to the forced Trainer exploration already tried. Agent57's bandit over exploration settings ([arXiv 2003.13350](https://arxiv.org/abs/2003.13350)) supports a population of pilots with spread entropy coefficients, which is cheap but contradicts the scaling argument: a bigger bonus lifts a 0.4% action little. Exploiters from AlphaStar's league ([Nature PDF copy](https://www.seas.upenn.edu/~cis520/papers/RL_for_starcraft.pdf)) supply states where rare plays matter, but the pilot already meets those states; its problem is credit, not coverage.

## Ten-game moves cannot see one-hundredth of a game

My arithmetic, not a measurement: with a paired-difference SD near 0.7, a 95% test for an effect of 0.01 needs about (2 x 0.7 / 0.01)^2, or **20,000 paired games** per move, roughly 2,000 times the 10 now used. A one-sided 1 SE rule passes about 16% of null moves, which matches the project's observed 15% of decks moving per iteration. So most accepted moves are probably noise, and the population drifts toward whatever the noise favours. The slight trimming of tempo cards (-0.07 a move) is the only lean large enough to be real, and it is a consistent signal that single-move tests can see only in aggregate.

Racing and best-arm methods do not rescue this. Successive halving and Hyperband spend budget adaptively, and Hyperband claims over an order-of-magnitude speedup against Bayesian optimisation on tuning problems ([Hyperband](https://arxiv.org/abs/1603.06560)). They can drop clearly bad moves early, but no schedule resolves a 0.01 effect at 10 games. Pooling can. BOCS fits a sparse linear model over binary variables and draws Thompson samples, with pairwise terms that scale poorly ([BOCS](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1806.08838), [scaling note](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2008.08177), snippets). A first-order version, one coefficient per card fitted on every deck's results, gives each rare card a wide posterior, so Thompson draws propose it at a rate set by its uncertainty. This is my adaptation; no source tests Thompson proposals for rarely present components, and the notes found no source on count bonuses or entropy floors for a REINFORCE proposer. Re-testing a passing move on fresh games is the adaptive re-evaluation idea from noisy evolutionary search ([Fieldsend](https://ore.exeter.ac.uk/repository/bitstream/10871/17039/1/EMO_2015_final_version_fieldsend.pdf), snippet). Hearthstone deck search uses about 200 games per deck ([MAP-Elites](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2112.03534), snippet), about 20 times this project's budget.

## Six experiments, ranked by evidence and cost

None uses human lists or human data in training. None is card-specific, so none needs Evan's approval under the hand-built-feature rule. Costs are rough and should go through the 30-minute-stage rule with a timed pilot.

| Rank | Change | Mechanism tested | Evidence | Cost | Readout that shows it worked |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Replace the pilot's policy update on the option head with an exponentiated-advantage (mirror) step, first on a single-state bandit with one rare high-value action, then in self-play | The pi(i) factor, not entropy, is what stops PlusPower from rising | Derivation from memory plus one snippet on mirror descent; none in card games | Bandit under 1 hour; one 30-minute pilot stage | Bandit: rare-action probability vs vanilla PPO at equal steps. Pilot: greedy PlusPower use at 10-HP breakpoints (now about 0.4%) and head-to-head share against the list-trained pilot (now 0.391, SE 0.013) |
| 2 | KL-Cov style throttle on updates for high-probability, positive-advantage plays | Dominant plays soak up probability that rare plays need | One LLM-RL snippet; no card-game evidence | One 30-minute stage, same harness as 1 | Same readouts; run in a separate arm from 1, since combining them hides which one acted |
| 3 | Fit a first-order Bayesian per-card model on all decks' paired results; draw one posterior sample per iteration and propose its best swaps instead of (or beside) the REINFORCE head | Pooling gives the per-card information that 10-game tests cannot | Method evidence is snippet-level; the budget argument is my arithmetic | Cheap: no extra games, a regression on logged results | Selection's per-card lean agrees across two seeds; rare-card proposal rate moves from 2-4% to a rate tied to posterior width; tempo and draw copies per deck against the lists' 9.2 and 6 |
| 4 | Re-test any move that passes the 1 SE rule on fresh games before keeping it | False accepts and drift | Adaptive re-evaluation idea (snippet); the 16% figure is arithmetic | Roughly +16% games plus the passing moves' re-test, on the order of 25% more | Fraction of kept moves whose second test is positive (near 50% would mean noise); fewer decks moving per iteration; stable tempo count |
| 5 | Add the remaining-deck composition (counts per card id of cards not yet seen) and thermometer encodings of HP, prizes and deck size as pilot inputs | Draw-card valuation needs deck information; Dominion's count gains | Thermometer: toy game, no variance. Deck counts: inferred from Cellar, not tested | Small code change, one 30-minute stage per arm | Head-to-head share vs 0.391 on coherent lists; draw-card use; flag that gains on Dominion were 20% to 40% on a six-card game and may not transfer |
| 6 | Train an exploiter pilot against the main pilot in the loop and add its games to training | Coverage of states where rare plays matter | Snippets from AlphaStar and ROA-Star; none for card games | The most expensive: a second learner | Rare-play frequency in exploiter-generated states; head-to-head vs 0.391 |

Experiments 1 and 2 test the same hypothesis through different means, and 3 and 4 share the drafting loop but fail independently, so run them as separate arms with their own seeds. The order follows evidence per cost, not size of possible gain. Per the null-by-default rule, a result counts only if it beats measured noise and replicates on a second seed against bands written before the run. The project's earlier attempts (forced exploration, reweighting, restarts, self-imitation, discounts, search distillation, higher draft entropy) all targeted data or supervision. None changed the update rule or the draft statistics.

## Conclusion

The reading across both literatures is that this project is failing on estimation geometry, not on exploration volume. The pilot's trouble is that its update gives a near-zero action almost no gradient, and the draft loop's trouble is that its test cannot see the effect sizes it is judged on. The cheap bandit test in experiment 1 can falsify the first explanation in under an hour, and the per-card regression in experiment 3 can falsify the second without playing a new game.

Two caveats limit confidence. The softmax identity is from memory, and most exploration sources were seen only as search snippets. And the Slay the Spire embedding result, if it holds, suggests that representation additions can raise offline numbers while lowering play strength, so every representation test (experiment 5) should be judged on head-to-head share, not on any imitation metric.
