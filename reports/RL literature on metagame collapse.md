# A Biased Judge Drives the Wall Collapse

The literature reads F1-F3 as one coupled failure, not three. A single shared pilot, trained mostly on the population's own decks, judges every edit, and an evaluator built that way rates highest whatever it already plays well. Robot co-design calls this the **first-mover advantage**. Weight-sharing architecture search measures it as rank correlations **near zero**. Lanctot's cross-play metric shows co-trained partners losing **up to 72%** of their return against strangers. On top of that judge, global max-average selection drains diversity within about ten generations (NEAT without speciation), and moving each deck to the best of a few noisy candidates keeps lucky edits (uncertain quality-diversity). Together these produce the relapse within 10-15 iterations and the 73% of moves made at the noise floor. Every fix metagame-rl has tried (type cap, frequent turnover, entropy floor, KL anchor, significance gate, a frozen pilot-v2 judge) matches a remedy the literature reports as partial on its own, and each gave the result that predicts: the deck shape held but the deck type did not. Beneath F1-F3 sits F4. The project's probes show the walls win slow prize races and deck-outs, and one-turn search on the human side wins those prize races back. The literature on training from scratch (AlphaStar at **149 against 1540 Elo** without human data; Czarnecki's spinning tops) says self-play without strong priors settles on strategies whose value needs little skill. The three expert-iteration runs failed exactly as Gumbel MuZero, Grill et al. and Muesli predict for hard argmax targets built from a few noisy simulations, and those papers give fixes that slot straight into the existing search. The ranked plan therefore strengthens the judge first: search-strength judging of edits, expert iteration with soft, bounded, gated targets, and better long-game value learning. Only then does it add structural selection fixes (pooled re-evaluation, a niche-conditioned draft head under local competition, AlphaStar's freeze-and-reset exploiters), which are only as good as the payoff matrix they read. No published system has this two-level structure of one shared pilot and a drafted population. Every effect size below is therefore an analogy until tested, and no card-game paper has rebuilt a human metagame's deck composition from self-play.

## One shared pilot rates highest whatever it has learned to play

The closest analogue to the deck loop is not game theory but robot co-design and neural architecture search, where one controller or one set of shared weights judges many candidate designs. Cheney and colleagues found that design search stalls long before control search. Freezing the body at any generation from 25 (of 5,000) onward made no significant difference to final fitness, and the best body appeared at generation 558 on average against 2,926 for the best controller ([Cheney et al. 2016](https://meclab.w3.uvm.edu/papers/2016_ALife_Cheney.pdf)). A controller specialised to the incumbent body makes every new body look worse at the moment it is judged. Protecting new bodies by "morphological age" raised distance travelled from **21.7 to 32.0 voxels**. Protecting controller innovations instead, with the same extra diversity, gave 22.0 (p = 0.240), no better than no protection ([Cheney et al. 2018](https://meclab.w3.uvm.edu/papers/2018_RoySoc_Cheney.pdf)). Mertan and Cheney call this a first-mover advantage. Early bodies accrue controller training and climb to their ceiling, while later bodies "can't survive long enough to accrue control optimization". A body-only search with a fixed, zero-parameter controller beat full co-optimisation in all four of their settings ([Mertan & Cheney 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.09231)). They later trained a controller for every body in a space of 1.3 million robots. Co-optimisation found a near-optimal body in only **13-22 of 100 runs**, and protecting new bodies "did not fix undervaluation" ([Mertan & Cheney, Artificial Life](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.17464)).

Weight-sharing architecture search shows the same bias at scale. Rankings with and without shared weights were "entirely uncorrelated" in one search space (Kendall τ = −0.004) and only weakly correlated in another (τ = 0.195), so state-of-the-art searches did about as well as random ones ([Yu et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/1902.08142)). FairNAS traced the cause to candidates with better early performance being sampled more and pulling further ahead. Giving every choice equal training ("strict fairness") raised τ to 0.74 ([Chu et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1907.01845)). Bilevel DARTS collapses onto parameter-free skip connections, and in one search space it chose a deliberately harmful noise operation for 5 of 8 operations ([Zela et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/1909.09656)). My reading is that Basic Energy plays the skip connection's role in deck space. Its value barely depends on pilot skill, while a Trainer's value depends on how well the shared pilot has learned to sequence it, so an under-trained shared judge over-selects Energy. An unreviewed 2026 preprint makes the general claim: judging a design before its controller finishes learning "favors fast learners" and narrows design diversity ([Song et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.23100)).

Card-game studies report the same dependence on the evaluating agent, though none quantifies it. Decks evolved under MetaStone's greedy AI beat human meta decks in all nine classes, 82.8% against 71.1% for the best human deck. But they were "basically all" aggro or mid-range, because that AI rarely trades, and one card recurred only because the AI could not punish it ([García-Sánchez et al. 2018](https://iris.polito.it/bitstream/11583/2706329/1/2018-kbsys.pdf)). Fontaine et al. state that their MAP-Elites deck maps are "dependent on a particular game-playing agent" ([Fontaine et al. 2019](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1904.10656)). Learned drafters are tied to the battle agent they trained with ([Vieira et al. 2020](https://www.sbgames.org/proceedings2020/ComputacaoFull/209690.pdf)). In competitive Pokémon battling, training on more teams makes the best single-team agent weaker and more exploitable but better on unseen teams ([Angliss et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.10326)).

Lanctot's joint-policy correlation gives a yardstick for this. Independently trained partners lose up to **71.7%** of the return that co-trained partners get, and training against mixtures of past partners cut that loss by 29-57 percentage points ([Lanctot et al. 2017](https://arxiv.org/abs/1711.00832)). The project's own matrix fits the pattern. The walls score 0.720 against the human lists when their population's pilot plays both sides, and 0.761 when pilot-v2 plays the human lists. They score 0.23-0.24 whenever pilot-v2 plays the walls, whoever plays the human lists. So the verdict follows whoever pilots the walls ([log #137](../notes/progress-log.md)). By Lanctot's formula that is a loss of about 0.68, close to his worst gridworld.

The same literature explains why the project's fixes changed the pilot but not the decks. Leader-follower learning theory requires the inner player, here the pilot, to adapt *faster* than the outer player, here the decks, and the outer step to use the inner player's reaction ([Fiez et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/1906.01217); [Hong et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2007.05170)). The KL anchor slowed the follower instead. It made the pilot about 65 Elo better on the human lists while the decks went on following the drafting ([log #138](../notes/progress-log.md)). Type caps and frequent turnover add diversity, and Cheney's controller-protection control shows that diversity alone is not the lever. A single frozen judge imposes its own style: García-Sánchez's greedy AI produced aggro decks, and pilot-v2 as a second judge pulled the decks "toward many Pokémon and long games, not toward the human types" ([log #134](../notes/progress-log.md)).

The literature offers three remedies instead. The first is **several judges of different skill**: Mahlmann et al. evaluated Dominion card sets with three agents of different skill and looked for sets that stay balanced regardless of skill ([Mahlmann et al. 2012](https://pure.itu.dk/en/publications/evolving-card-sets-towards-balancing-dominion/)). The second is **a stronger, less exploitable judge**: in Legends of Code and Magic, search added 24.6 points of win rate to the same network and cut its exploitability by 21-28 points ([Rubin 2026](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.06816)). The third is **training the shared learner on the designs it plays worst**, as PLR⊥ does by updating only on high-regret levels ([Jiang et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.02439)) and MetaMorph does by sampling poorly performing morphologies more often ([Gupta et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.11931)).

One caution cuts the other way. In a simple card game, stronger MCTS agents legitimately favoured high-health, low-attack cards ([Zook et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1908.01423)). Walls rising with skill is therefore not proof of a bug on its own; the human-list ground truth is what flags it.

## Global, noisy selection drains every niche within about ten iterations

Once a judge prefers one type, the selection rule decides how fast the population follows, and the evolutionary literature says it follows fast. Without speciation, NEAT "quickly converges on whatever topology happens to initially perform best", draining diversity "within 10 generations". It needed **7× more evaluations and failed 25% of the time**, where full NEAT needed 3,600 and never failed ([Stanley & Miikkulainen 2002](https://nn.cs.utexas.edu/downloads/papers/stanley.ec02.pdf)). Novelty search with global competition converged on a single body plan. Local competition, where an individual only has to beat its nearest neighbours, kept the niches ([Lehman & Stanley 2011](https://groups.csail.mit.edu/EVO-DesignOpt/gecco2011Proceedings/proceedings/p211.pdf)). Lanctot names the game-theoretic version: fictitious play over a uniform mixture "can only sharpen the meta-strategy distribution by repeatedly generating the same best responses" ([Lanctot et al. 2017](https://arxiv.org/abs/1711.00832)). AlphaStar replaced max-average opponent sampling with prioritised fictitious self-play because "the pursuit of the mean win-rate might lead to policies that are easy to exploit" ([Vinyals et al. 2019](https://storage.googleapis.com/deepmind-media/research/alphastar/AlphaStar_unformatted.pdf)).

The metagame-rl loop moves each deck to whichever edit scores better against the whole population. That is per-lineage crowding under global, max-average competition, the configuration all three sources predict will collapse. Czarnecki et al. add that training converges only once the population covers a whole Nash cluster at its skill level, and that it cycles below a critical population size ([Czarnecki et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2004.09468)). A 99-deck population that has collapsed to one type has an effective size of one. The project's earlier PSRO run with a Nash meta-solver collapsed to a single deck. Balduzzi et al. predict exactly that whenever one agent dominates the payoff matrix ([Balduzzi et al. 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1901.08106)).

Noise speeds up the drift. Elitist archives under noisy fitness fill with "lucky sub-performing solutions" ([Flageat & Cully 2023](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2302.00463)). Choosing the best of several noisy estimates is biased upward, the optimizer's curse ([Smith & Winkler 2006](https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.1050.0451)), and that is the 73% of decks that moved when no candidate was truly better. The benchmarked fixes keep several candidates per niche and keep re-evaluating them, so lucky ones decay back to their true value. Keeping 32 candidates per niche (Deep-Grid) was best in three of four tasks, and re-evaluating the whole archive every generation was best in the fourth ([Flageat & Cully 2023](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2302.00463)). Enhanced POET requires a challenger to beat the maximum of the incumbent's last five scores. That matched the original's results with 79.7% of the compute and cut replacements from 50.4% to 22.3% ([Wang et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2003.08536)).

The project's significance gate did what a noise filter can do. Moves fell from 65-83 an iteration to 13-28, and the largest type stayed at 12-26 decks against the control's 33-74. But each move it accepted still added 0.7-2.5 Pokémon and cut 0.2-1.9 Trainers, and 73 of 99 decks still held both Chansey and Snorlax ([log #140](../notes/progress-log.md)). A gate removes noise, not the judge's bias.

The draft head's entropy collapse has a named counterpart. In PGA-MAP-Elites, a policy-gradient variation operator trained on global fitness pushes every offspring toward the single fitness optimum. Conditioning its critic and actor on a target descriptor, so offspring improve "while staying in their cell", raised QD-score by **82%** ([Faldor et al. 2023](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2303.03832)). The REINFORCE head is such an operator, which is why a fresh head drafts every new deck into Chansey/Snorlax within a few iterations whatever the type cap does ([log #130](../notes/progress-log.md)). ByteDance's Hearthstone agent showed the same collapse in a learned upstream choice: its hero policy "collapsed to one hero". The fix was to remove the choice (heroes sampled uniformly) and to force random early deck picks during training ([Xiao et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2303.05197)).

The exploiter heads' drift to the main type matches Balduzzi's warning that training an agent against what beats it contracts the population: "training rock against paper would make it more like scissors" ([Balduzzi et al. 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1901.08106)). AlphaStar's answer is procedural. An exploiter that wins less than 20% plays half its games against older, weaker snapshots of the main agent. It is frozen into the league once it beats the main agents more than 70% of the time, and is then reset. Adding main and league exploiters raised main-agent Elo from **1540 to 1693 to 1824** and league robustness from 6% to 35% to 62% ([Vinyals et al. 2019](https://storage.googleapis.com/deepmind-media/research/alphastar/AlphaStar_unformatted.pdf)). The project's exploiters had neither the curriculum nor the freeze-and-reset.

Diversity bonuses carry a caveat. Payoff-row diversity is the expected size of a determinantal point process (DPP) over the matchup matrix. It costs O(n³), provably enlarges the set of reachable strategies, and reached the lowest exploitability on the 888-policy AlphaStar meta-game while using fewer than 50 of its policies ([Perez-Nieves et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.07927)). It is a different measure from the pilot-embedding novelty bonus tried in log #43-47, which helped "modestly and not reliably" ([log #46](../notes/progress-log.md)). It rewards a deck for beating different opponents, not for looking different. Enhanced POET's PATA-EC describes an environment by how a panel of agents ranks on it, needs no hand-coded features, and costs about 82% more compute ([Wang et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2003.08536)). Yet every one of these measures reads a payoff matrix produced by the biased judge. My inference from the first section is that diversity and niching fixes will keep holding the shape but not the type until the judge stops favouring decks that need little skill.

## Expert iteration failed exactly as few-simulation theory predicts

The project's three distillation runs scored up to 8 options per probe, each by 2-4 re-dealt lines valued by the pilot at the end of the turn, and trained toward the best option as a hard one-hot target at a weight of up to 1.0. They lost **79, 56 and 37 Elo**. The targets disagreed with the policy on 60-70% of probes, and policy entropy rose by 0.27-0.29 ([log #103](../notes/progress-log.md), [log #106](../notes/progress-log.md)). Each symptom has a published cause.

Danihelka et al. show that AlphaZero-style training "can fail to improve its policy network, if not visiting all actions at the root". They give a three-action example in which the best of two sampled actions does worse than the policy itself. MuZero "fails to learn from 16 or less simulations" on 9x9 Go, while Gumbel MuZero "learns reliably even with 2" ([Danihelka et al. 2022](https://openreview.net/forum?id=bERaNdoegnO)). Grill et al. show that at five simulations the gain comes mainly from replacing visit counts with a KL-regularised target ([Grill et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2007.12509)). Muesli's clipped-advantage target bounds how far any one noisy batch can move the policy: the total-variation distance is at most tanh(c/2) ([Hessel et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2104.06159)). Soemers et al. note that a policy trained by cross-entropy toward search targets inherits their spread ([Soemers et al. 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1905.05809)), which accounts for the rise in entropy. SPARTA plays the blueprint's own action unless search beats it by a threshold (0.05 in Hanabi), which "improves the total score even for a large number of rollouts". It also stops spending rollouts on actions more than two standard deviations behind ([Lerer et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/1912.02318)). KataGo found that search "usually does not deviate much from the policy prior" unless the budget is large, so it recorded targets only from full searches, on 25% of turns ([Wu 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1902.10565)).

The leaf value is a second, separate fault. Gumbel's guarantee holds only "when action-values are correctly evaluated". Log #103 found that lines scored by a weak end-of-turn value favoured immediate plays (Trainers, retreating) over development (benching Pokémon) ([log #103](../notes/progress-log.md)). Depth is not the fix. In MuZero ablations, shallow trees with simple Monte Carlo rollouts were "as performant as more complex methods" ([Hamrick et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2011.04021)). In Legends of Code and Magic, "most of the value of the search is immediately gained by evaluating plans" ([Rubin 2026](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.06816)). What needs fixing is the target and the value that scores the lines, not the horizon.

Better value learning is the other lever for long games, and the project has already ruled out one suspect. Discounting cannot explain the stalling, because the pilot has always trained with γ = 1 ([log #131](../notes/progress-log.md)), the setting ByteDance found worth about 7% of win rate in Hearthstone ([Xiao et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2303.05197)). With GAE λ = 0.95 and median games of 53 turns, credit for an early tempo play arrives almost entirely through the value estimate, since GAE amounts to shaping with the value function as the potential ([Schulman et al. 2016](https://arxiv.org/abs/1506.02438)). Three card-game and Go results point at the value function. DouZero chose **Monte Carlo value targets** because their convergence "is not impacted by the episode length", and ranked first of 344 DouDizhu agents on four GPUs ([Zha et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2106.06135)). PerfectDou gave its **critic the hidden cards** during training only and needed about a tenth of DouZero's samples. In its ablation, the hidden-information critic without the game-specific dense reward scored 0.540 against DouZero where vanilla PPO scored 0.346, though the extracted table's layout is slightly ambiguous ([Yang et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.16406)). KataGo's **auxiliary outcome targets**, final board ownership and score, give "direct feedback" on what caused a loss, and removing them "resulted in a noticeable drop in learning efficiency" ([Wu 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1902.10565)).

The project tested λ = 1 for the policy within a turn (−37 Elo, [log #106](../notes/progress-log.md)). It has not tried Monte Carlo value targets, a hidden-information critic, or auxiliary heads that predict each player's final counters. Prize shaping cannot be the way out: it is potential-based, and such shaping leaves the optimal policy unchanged ([Ng et al. 1999](https://people.eecs.berkeley.edu/~russell/papers/icml99-shaping.pdf)).

Search is more useful as a judge than as a teacher, at least to begin with. One-turn search on the human side already cut the walls' score from 0.648 to 0.277 in games not ended by deck-out ([log #139](../notes/progress-log.md)). In Rubin's ablations, the same network went from 26.8% to 51.4% against ByteRL once it searched over plans ([Rubin 2026](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.06816)). None of the card agents trained from scratch to strong play used play-time search, but all spent far more compute than one desktop has. DouZero trained for 30 days on four GPUs. ByteRL used 24 V100s with 600 CPU cores each for 72 hours. The Hearthstone agent used 24 V100s and 5,856 cores for 23 days ([Zha et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2106.06135); [Xi et al. 2023](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2303.04096); [Xiao et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2303.05197)). Sound imperfect-information search is out of reach: ReBeL used up to 128 machines with 8 GPUs each for data generation ([Brown et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2007.13544)). Cheap determinized search is unsound in theory, but it has a Magic: The Gathering precedent ([Cowling, Ward & Powley 2012](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/75050/)) and is the affordable substitute for compute.

## From scratch, self-play settles on strategies that need no skill

F5 is the regime where the population literature shows failure even at scale. AlphaStar's full league scored a test Elo of 1540, and the same system without human data scored **149**; its exploiters were reset to human-imitation parameters ([Vinyals et al. 2019](https://storage.googleapis.com/deepmind-media/research/alphastar/AlphaStar_unformatted.pdf)). Czarnecki et al. explain why: priors such as search or imitation start training in higher parts of the spinning top, where less non-transitivity has to be resolved ([Czarnecki et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2004.09468)). Co-design shows the low-skill attractor directly. In seven of eight seeds, a controller learned the "safer" option of standing still, and the design distribution moved toward bodies that made locomotion harder; only reward shaping fixed it ([Schaff et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1801.01432)).

The project's from-scratch runs land in the matching place: a no-attack, deck-out equilibrium of Pokémon piles ([log #124](../notes/progress-log.md)). Even with γ = 0.995, the median deck went from 12 to 25 Pokémon and deck-outs from 61% to 85% of games within five iterations ([log #132](../notes/progress-log.md)). A frozen pilot-v2 drafting from wide decks also heads for Pokémon piles ([log #131](../notes/progress-log.md)).

The literature's general remedies for this regime start with an affordable prior, here search used as the judge. Perfect information during training only made Suphx's normal agent improve "much faster than standard RL", though Suphx also used human logs ([Li et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2003.13590)). POET admits only designs that are neither too easy nor too hard for the current agents, a minimal criterion that would keep zero-win piles out ([Wang et al. 2019](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1901.01753)). DeNA's Pokémon TCG Pocket AI trained on practice decks that keep evolution chains intact and include rarely played cards more often, so the pilot learns to use them ([Inven Global](https://www.invenglobal.com/articles/24133/how-reinforcement-learning-ai-tackles-the-complex-rules-of-pok%C3%A9mon-trading-card-game-pocket)). DeNA's generator also adjusted Energy counts, which would count as a game-specific prior here.

No paper addresses F6 directly, but reward theory explains the null result. A turn limit that scores unfinished games as draws is not potential-based, so it changes the game's optimum, not just the speed of learning it ([Ng et al. 1999](https://people.eecs.berkeley.edu/~russell/papers/icml99-shaping.pdf)). Draws also pull payoff entries toward 0.5. That shrinks the differences that Nash support, effective diversity and DPP diversity all depend on. The project saw both effects: at 40 turns, up to 94% of games ended at the limit, and at 70 and 120 turns the decks relapsed like the control ([log #142](../notes/progress-log.md); [log #143](../notes/progress-log.md)).

One project finding qualifies the ground truth itself. The human lists were built for 20-minute rounds in which an unfinished game went to the player with more Prizes for two-thirds of a win's points. Re-scored under that rule, with search on the human side, the walls took only 0.40-0.49 of the points. That is approximate, since prize counts at the cut were stood in for by counts at game end ([log #139](../notes/progress-log.md)). A plain-draw limit does not reproduce that payoff, so part of the human lists' optimality rests on a rule the environment lacks. Whether the environment should include the tournament rule is a choice about the game's definition that the literature cannot settle. Evan has directed the work to the learning process ([log #141](../notes/progress-log.md)).

The first wave fits the three-screen limit: ranks 1, 2 and the `--lam-value 1` arm of rank 3 in parallel, with rank 8's logging added to all three. If search-strength judging moves the decks off the walls, the biased-judge diagnosis holds, and ranks 2 and 3 become the way to make the training pilot as strong as the judge. If the walls survive it, two explanations remain: the engine lacks the tournament clock, or the walls are genuinely strong when games can run without limit. That is a question for Evan, not for more selection machinery.

Several heavier ideas are left out on purpose. ReBeL- or Student-of-Games-style sound search needs hundreds of GPUs or TPUs. A full league of separate pilots would repeat the PFSP pool of past pilots dropped at log #138, at AlphaStar's 12-learner scale. Stronger prize shaping cannot move the optimum. More turn limits, a single frozen judge, Nash-PSRO alone and raw fitness sharing have each been tried or ruled out in the log.

## Conclusion

The literature changes the order of the work more than its content. The project has treated the deck population and the draft heads as the place where collapse happens. The remedies tried there behaved as the co-design and architecture-search literature predicts for a biased evaluator: each slowed the drift and none changed its direction. Every diversity, niching or game-theoretic method in this review reads a payoff matrix, and in metagame-rl that matrix is written by a pilot that plays prize races badly and walls well. The judge sits upstream of the drafter, so fixing it is not one item among many but the condition for the others to work.

The second change concerns search. The card agents that reached strong play from scratch paid for it with dozens of GPUs and thousands of CPU cores, and AlphaStar without human data shows what happens with neither a prior nor imitation. On one desktop, cheap determinized search is the only substitute available. So far the project has used it where the literature says it is weakest (distillation into hard targets) rather than where it is strongest (evaluation, and soft, gated policy improvement). The project's validation, deck-composition statistics plus head-to-head play against 24 tournament lists, is more quantitative than anything published for card games, so a clean negative result from search-strength judging would be a finding in its own right. It would place the remaining gap in the game's definition rather than in the learning process.
