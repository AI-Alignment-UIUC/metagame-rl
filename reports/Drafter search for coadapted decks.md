# Testing block moves before learning linkage

No source we found shows that crossover, linkage learning or a learned block proposer finds co-adapted card sets under noisy win/loss fitness, so the first tests should be cheap block-level operators judged against a mutation-only control. The evidence is thin. The linkage-learning and Bayesian-optimization papers were read as abstracts or search summaries, with no effect sizes or evaluation budgets. The card-deck genetic-algorithm papers (Hearthstone, Magic) agree on one point: naive crossover breaks colour and mana consistency, so any recombination needs a repair step. The best-supported learned route is a pairwise-term surrogate in the style of BOCS, but it has not been tested on noisy win/loss outcomes. The most useful first operators are a count-level crossover (take each card name's count from one parent, then repair to 60 cards and 4 copies) and a block swap (replace a whole Pokemon line plus its Energy). Both need repair, and both should be accepted only with a noise-aware rule. Everything below that rests on memory or on search snippets is marked.

## Linkage learners look promising but the evidence is abstract-only

The only linkage-learning genetic algorithm checked is DSMGA-II. It builds a dependency matrix from pairwise linkage tests and then recombines whole linked groups ("restricted mixing", "back mixing"). Its abstract reports fewer function evaluations than LT-GOMEA and hBOA on trap, NK-landscape, Ising and MAX-SAT problems, but it gives no numbers, no population sizes and no comparison with a mutation-only genetic algorithm, and the abstract does not discuss noise ([arXiv 1807.11669](https://arxiv.org/abs/1807.11669)). A search snippet (not the full text) says pairwise detection was chosen partly for resistance to sampling noise. The full paper's tables were not read, so no budget can be quoted. Noisy-fitness results for the GOMEA family were not found at all; a 2024 parameterless GOMEA paper and a 2019 thesis on optimal-mixing population sizing appeared only in search results. Royal-road and building-block results from the 1990s are from memory and unverified. The honest reading is that linkage learning is the right idea for "few names at high copy counts, Energy matching its Pokemon", but we have no evidence of its cost or of how it behaves with a win/loss signal.

## Noise is handled by sampling theory, which gives conflicting advice

Selection under noise has an old theory. Miller and Goldberg (1996) model how noise changes convergence under several selection schemes, using a conservative population-size predictor ([abstract](https://www.bohrium.com/paper-details/genetic-algorithms-selection-schemes-and-the-varying-effects-of-noise/811933076908146689-2689)). Rattray and Shapiro's critique states that under weak selection, Gaussian noise can be removed by enlarging the population ([core.ac.uk](https://core.ac.uk/download/78877694.pdf)). Resampling theory says repeats cut the noise standard deviation roughly with the square root of the repeat count ([Liu, Saint-Pierre and Teytaud](https://core.ac.uk/download/49648739.pdf)), that fixed or polynomially growing repeat counts can still converge ([Cauwet](https://core.ac.uk/download/49651762.pdf)), and that adaptive sampling can beat fixed sampling ([Qian et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1810.05045)). Results on Noisy OneMax conflict: one analysis found resampling useless, another that the best resampling rate rises with dimension ([arXiv 1706.05086](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1706.05086)). Most of these were seen as search summaries, not full text.

For our loop the arithmetic is the constraint (my estimate, not a measurement). With win/loss outcomes at p = 0.5, the standard error of a 10-game mean is about 0.16, and about 0.045 at 125 games, before any paired-design variance reduction. Single-card edits move win rate by far less than that band, which explains the missing signal. A block move that changes many cards at once can produce a larger true effect, so it is more likely to clear the noise band at the same game budget. That is a reason to try block moves first, though no source confirms it for card games.

## Card-game genetic algorithms warn about repair but offer no head-to-head

The deck-building literature checked is mostly search summaries. Hearthstone and Magic work agrees that naive crossover breaks colour and mana consistency, so repair or colour-aware operators matter ([Garcia-Sanchez et al., Torino 2018](https://iris.polito.it/bitstream/11583/2706329/1/2018-kbsys.pdf)). An Arena deckbuilding study restricts crossover and mutation to "active" genes and uses uniform crossover with a constant mutation rate as its baseline ([arXiv 2001.01326](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2001.01326)). The Torino work also adds a mutation operator modelled on how human players edit decks, which our no-human-priors rule forbids in training. A 2017 NTNU Magic thesis used a genetic algorithm judged by expert analysis rather than by play ([NTNU](https://ntnuopen.ntnu.no/ntnu-xmlui/handle/11250/2462429)). We found no card-game comparison of crossover against mutation-only search with numbers, so our own control run would be the first such test.

## Learned block proposals rest on BOCS, with no noisy-outcome evidence

BOCS fits a second-order binary model (linear plus pairwise terms) with a horseshoe prior and Thompson sampling, and its abstract claims it consistently beats combinatorial and Bayesian-optimization baselines, with no specifics ([Baptista and Poloczek](https://arxiv.org/abs/1806.08838)). The scalable variant reports that one-hot encoding categorical variables creates many binary variables, tests only on four synthetic benchmarks averaged over 10 runs, and cites others for COMBO beating BOCS on complex domains ([Deshwal et al.](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2008.08177)). A 277-card pool one-hot encoded is exactly the large regime flagged as costly. A pairwise co-occurrence surrogate fitted to the population's own top decks, used as a prior over group changes, needs no human decks and is the cheapest learned option, but with about 125 paired games per decision it would depend on a strong shrinkage prior. GFlowNet and set-generator methods were not checked, and none of the sources tests a noisy win/loss objective.

## Large-step moves are untested here, and agent-play signals were not found

Our "swap all copies of one card" move is already a mid-sized step. The sources on macro-mutation and consolidation moves were not reached; Kernighan-Lin style variable-depth search is from memory and unverified. Pidgeon (2025) reports only that different fitness functions yield different Hearthstone decks (abstract seen, [source](https://pats.cs.cf.ac.uk/!archive_desc?p=1321)), and "Evolving the Hearthstone Meta" evolves card attributes, not decks ([arXiv 1907.01623](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1907.01623)). No source on game-agnostic agent-play signals (dead-card rate, unused resources, play against a passive opponent) turned up; that is a gap in this search, not proof that none exists.

## Conclusion

Operators worth testing first, in order, are these. First, a count-level crossover between two top decks with repair, against a mutation-only control. Second, a compound line swap (a Pokemon name with its Energy), which targets the measured overspread of 6.6 Pokemon names versus 4.6 and 3.7 Energy types versus 1.8 in the human lists. Third, a consolidation move that merges two names into one, scored only by the agent's own selection; hard-coding Energy counts would be a game-specific prior. Fourth, a BOCS-style pairwise surrogate over group changes, only if the first three show signal. Each should be judged against a band written before the run, with a second seed, because the noise estimate above is larger than any single-card effect.

The main change in understanding is that the shortfall is a measurement problem as much as a search problem: no operator can be ranked until its effect is larger than a standard error of about 0.045 at 125 games. The next reading, by priority, is the full DSMGA-II tables and any noisy-fitness GOMEA work, then macro-mutation and consolidation moves, GFlowNet set generators, and agent-play signals.
