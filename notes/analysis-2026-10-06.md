# Where A5 stands, and what to try next (2026-10-06)

An analysis of progress-log entries #30-42, combined with a literature survey of the RL methods
that address what those runs found. Every number below comes from the log. Paper summaries come
from their abstracts and were checked to exist; the ones marked *unverified* were not looked up.

## What the runs established

**1. The pilot decides what every deck builder can see.** Every way of judging a deck goes
through the play policy: real games, the matchup model's training labels, the value head, and the
game statistics. Three pilots have now been measured:

| Pilot | vs SimpleBot (archived decks) | vs SimpleBot (unseen random decks) | Deck-outs | Spread across #36's four variants |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| a4-tok (trained on the 24 human lists) | 92.6% | — | 44% | — |
| Result #1's pilot (a4-tok, deck-out wins paid 0) | 93.0% | 55.5% | 27% | **18 points** (49.9% → 68.1%) |
| Scratch pilot, log #42 (random decks only) | 78.7% | **67.3%** | **19%** | **2 points** (60.5% → 62.3%) |

- The pilot trained on human lists is strong on those lists, but it is biased. Its value head
  overrates Energy-heavy decks (#36), and it generalizes poorly to decks it has not seen.
- The human-free pilot fixes both problems:
  - its value is calibrated, within 0.03 of the real results, and rates Graham's list highest;
  - it handles new decks better (+12 points on unseen random decks).
- But it is weaker, and deck choice barely changes its results: the four variants spread over 2
  points instead of 18. A weak pilot gives the builder little signal, because every deck plays
  about the same in its hands. Deck differences only become visible above a skill threshold.

**2. Search exploits whatever scores it.** Each scorer failed in its own way:

| Scorer | What it did |
|---|---|
| Matchup model | Predicted 0.75-0.90, real 0-0.6 (#34) |
| Pilot's value | Drifted to 30+ Energy, the region its value overrates (#36) |
| Matchup model retrained on the search's decks | Learned the same bias, because the data never held a coherent Trainer-heavy deck (#37) |
| Deck-strength model, 16 games per candidate | Accurate (error 0.046) but scored only 1,740 candidates, too few to find anything (#39) |

**3. Closing the loop helps strength, not bias.** Fine-tuning the pilot on each iteration's decks
produced the first built mixture to beat the human field (60.7%, #40).

- Its deck still runs ~30 Energy, and the pilot co-adapts to it (+11 points under the pilot
  trained on its games).
- Sampling with τ = 0.03 kept the decks human-like and the equilibrium diverse, but weaker
  (33-43%, #41).
- Each of these is a single seed.

**4. Human priors still in the pipeline** (#42 removed the human-trained pilot):

- The card pool is limited to the 56 cards the field played.
- `rl/psro.py`'s random starting decks use a 16-22 Energy shape.
- The log #39 strength model was fitted with archived lists. It is not used in the main
  pipeline.

## What the literature says about these failures

**Searching a learned scorer: overoptimization.** The gap between predicted and real results is
the falling half of the overoptimization curve in Gao, Schulman & Hilton (ICML 2023,
arXiv:2210.10760): the real objective rises, then falls, as search pressure against a proxy
grows. Three established fixes, all card-agnostic:

- **Pessimism for acceptance, optimism only for deciding what to test.** Coste et al. (ICLR 2024,
  arXiv:2310.02743) found that optimizing an ensemble's worst case or mean − λ·variance largely
  removes overoptimization. We used mean + spread (optimism) both to pick candidates and to accept
  them, which is backwards for acceptance.
- **Trust regions.** TuRBO (Eriksson et al., NeurIPS 2019, arXiv:1910.01739) caps how far the
  search may move from decks with real results, and grows or shrinks that radius as real games
  confirm or contradict the scorer.
- **Retrain the scorer where the search is going.** Autofocused oracles (Fannjiang & Listgarten,
  NeurIPS 2020, arXiv:2006.08052) and Conservative Objective Models (Trabucco et al., ICML 2021,
  arXiv:2107.06882) do this. Deep Surrogate Assisted MAP-Elites for Hearthstone deckbuilding
  (Zhang et al., GECCO 2022, arXiv:2112.03534), the closest existing system to ours, always replays
  the surrogate's favourite decks in real games and retrains on them. That closes the
  contamination loop of #37.

**The value head off its training decks.** Deep ensembles keep their calibration under dataset
shift best of the methods compared (Ovadia et al., NeurIPS 2019, arXiv:1906.02530). They give a
cheap way to see when the value is out of its depth. KataGo (Wu 2019, arXiv:1902.10565) used
auxiliary prediction targets to make its value head far more robust. Card-agnostic targets here
would be the prize difference at the end, turns remaining, and the opponent's next action.

**Curricula over decks.** Unsupervised environment design treats the deck as the environment.

- Robust PLR (Jiang et al., NeurIPS 2021, arXiv:2110.02439) replays the levels with the highest
  regret; for us, the decks where the value head's error is largest.
- ACCEL (Parker-Holder et al., ICML 2022, arXiv:2203.01302) also mutates those high-regret levels.
  It runs on one desktop GPU, and its mutations map directly onto card swaps.

Together these are a principled replacement for both a hand-designed random-deck shape and
per-iteration piloting.

**Self-play blind spots and rare decisive moves.**

- AlphaStar's league (Vinyals et al., Nature 2019) adds exploiters, policies trained only to beat
  the main agent, to expose shared blind spots.
- Self-Imitation Learning (Oh et al., ICML 2018, arXiv:1806.05635) reinforces an agent's own past
  actions that did better than expected. It is cheap and has no domain knowledge.
- RUDDER (Arjona-Medina et al., NeurIPS 2019, arXiv:1806.07857) redistributes delayed reward to
  the decisive steps. It is heavier.
- DeepNash (Perolat et al., Science 2022, arXiv:2206.15378) uses R-NaD regularization, which damps
  cycling and passivity without search in a large imperfect-information game.
- The closest card-game result is a single end-to-end policy for both the draft and the battle in
  Legends of Code and Magic (Xi et al. 2023, arXiv:2303.04096).

**Diversity without losing strength.**

- Diversity terms on the *payoff* space keep strength better than randomness in what is picked.
  Examples are the DPP term of Diverse PSRO (Perez-Nieves et al., ICML 2021, arXiv:2103.07927)
  and the Nash-aligned metric of PSD-PSRO (Yao et al., NeurIPS 2023). Our τ = 0.03 sampling is
  the latter kind.
- DvD (Parker-Holder et al., NeurIPS 2020, arXiv:2002.00632) adapts the diversity weight online,
  so it drops when diversity stops paying.
- α-Rank (Omidshafiei et al., Sci. Rep. 2019, arXiv:1903.01373) keeps rock-paper-scissors cycles
  as a group instead of collapsing to one deck, which is useful alongside Nash.

**Prior work on this exact game.** No peer-reviewed RL work on Pokémon TCG deck building and
play was found, so this project appears to be new ground.

**Checked against the self-discovery rule.** The survey also suggested MAP-Elites with
Energy-fraction and Trainer-fraction axes, and randomizing Energy counts during pilot training.

- The randomized Energy counts are what #42 already did; they name no card.
- Axes defined by card types would steer the search toward the very balance it should discover,
  so they are out. Play-statistic axes (decisions per game, game length) are fine.

## Next steps, ranked

1. **A stronger human-free pilot.** Everything downstream depends on pilot strength (finding 1).
   - First, fix the league slowdown: collection grew from 7 s to 55 s an iteration, because each
     snapshot is its own model and splits the batches. Batching snapshots together, or one
     network conditioned on a snapshot index as in NeuPL (Liu et al., ICLR 2022,
     arXiv:2202.07415), makes longer runs affordable.
   - Then train longer with a larger token model (#25 found it underfits), and add
     self-imitation and KataGo-style auxiliary heads.
   - Measure success by the spread across #36's variants, not only by SimpleBot.
   - Cost: a few hours of engineering, then one long run (~6-8 h).
2. **Safeguards for searching the scorer.**
   - Accept by pessimism (ensemble mean − κ·std), cap distance from the last real-tested deck
     with a trust region, and keep testing finalists with real games.
   - Plot real win rate against search pressure to find the overoptimization peak.
   - Cost: a day of coding; little compute.
3. **A regret-curated deck curriculum for the pilot (PLR/ACCEL).** Keep a buffer of decks the
   value head misjudges most, by |value − result| or ensemble disagreement. Replay and mutate them
   in pilot training. This replaces per-iteration piloting and the hand-made random-deck shape
   with something learned. Cost: medium.
4. **Response diversity with an adaptive weight, instead of temperature.** Score candidates by
   win rate against the mixture plus λ times the DPP gain on the payoff matrix, with λ adapted as
   in DvD. Report α-Rank next to Nash. Cost: about a day; trivial compute.
5. **Replication.** Two more seeds of result #1 and of both closed loops, once 1-2 are in place.
   No ranking here is settled on one seed.
6. **Remove the last priors.**
   - Move to the full card pool, with uniform random starting decks or the curriculum from 3.
   - Flag decks with extreme win rates and odd game statistics (very short games, unusual
     endings) as likely engine bugs, before the builder treats them as strategies.
   - Cost: engine verification first (the README's A5.3 note).

Dropped for now: refitting the matchup model with Conservative Objective Models. Logs #37-38
showed that what limits it is the data, not the model, and that game statistics carry the signal.
Exploiter leagues (AlphaStar) are worth adding to item 1 if self-imitation is not enough.
