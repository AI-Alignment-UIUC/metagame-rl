# Population-based training and game-theoretic methods against strategy collapse in self-play

Scope: why self-play and shared-population training collapse to one strategy, which population methods keep several strategies alive and keep agents general, and how each maps onto metagame-rl's failure modes F1-F6. Primary papers were read in full text (PDF to text) unless marked "abstract only". Project history is cited from the repo's own notes so that suggestions are not re-proposals of things already tried.

## PSRO and its variants (Lanctot 2017, Pipeline/Rectified PSRO, diversity-aware PSRO, Nash averaging, alpha-Rank): what failure modes do they address and how expensive are they?

### Takeaway
PSRO generalises self-play, fictitious play and double oracle by training each new policy against a *meta-strategy* (a mixture over the existing population chosen by a game-theoretic solver). The choice of meta-solver decides the failure mode: self-play cycles, uniform fictitious play wastes effort and "sharpens" onto repeated best responses, a Nash meta-solver degenerates to self-play once one agent dominates, and rectified Nash grows niches but has no convergence guarantee. The expensive part is the oracle (one RL training run per added policy) plus O(n²) payoff estimation. For a deck population the oracle is a deck edit, not an RL run, so the solver side costs almost nothing.

### Cited Findings
- **PSRO (Lanctot, Zambaldi, Gruslys, Lazaridou, Tuyls, Pérolat, Silver, Graepel; NeurIPS 2017).** Each epoch adds, for each player, an approximate best response ("oracle") trained by deep RL against opponents sampled from the current meta-strategy. Missing payoff entries are then filled by simulation, and a meta-solver recomputes the mixture. Independent RL, fictitious play, iterated best response and double oracle are special cases (meta-strategy = latest policy, uniform over past policies, and Nash respectively). — [Lanctot et al. 2017](https://arxiv.org/abs/1711.00832)
- Lanctot et al. name the failure modes of the classic solvers. Fictitious play "can only sharpen the meta-strategy distribution by repeatedly generating the same best responses". Responses to a Nash meta-strategy (double oracle) "overfit to a specific equilibrium" in general-sum games and cannot generalise to parts of the space no equilibrium reaches. Their compromise is meta-strategies with full support that mix in γ-exploration: projected replicator dynamics keeps every policy's probability ≥ γ/(K+1). — [Lanctot et al. 2017](https://arxiv.org/abs/1711.00832)
- Cost of PSRO: computing the empirical payoff tensor "takes time exponential in |Π|" in the number of players. The parallel version, Deep Cognitive Hierarchies (DCH), runs n·K processes (n players, K levels), and each process stores nK policies, so memory is O(n²K²). — [Lanctot et al. 2017](https://arxiv.org/abs/1711.00832)
- **Rectified Nash response, PSRO_rN (Balduzzi, Garnelo, Bachrach, Czarnecki, Pérolat, Jaderberg, Graepel; ICML 2019).** Each agent with support under the Nash of the population is trained against the Nash-weighted mixture of the agents it *beats or ties*, to "amplify their strengths and ignore their weaknesses". When one agent dominates, the Nash puts all its mass on that agent and PSRO_rN degenerates to self-play against the best agent. Its pathological mode is "many extremely local niches", which yields "a large population of highly specialized agents". — [Balduzzi et al. 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1901.08106)
- PSRO_N (response to the Nash) fails when the population's gamescape already contains the game's Nash (e.g. any rock-paper-scissors subpopulation of the disc game), because no better response to the Nash exists. "If the game is transitive then PSRO_N degenerates to self-play." — [Balduzzi et al. 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1901.08106)
- Results in Balduzzi et al. 2019, under a fixed oracle-query budget, with relative population performance as the metric: PSRO_rN outperforms the others "across a wide range of allowed compute budgets", PSRO_U and PSRO_N are comparable, and self-play is worst. Upgraded to a population (all its past agents), self-play is still worst in differentiable Lotto, but in Blotto it "slightly outperforms PSRO_N and PSRO_U". PSRO_rN is the only method that grows the convex hull of the empirical gamescape at every iteration. The games were Blotto (3 areas, 10 coins, 1000 games) and differentiable Lotto (9 customers, 500 games). — [Balduzzi et al. 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1901.08106)
- **Pipeline PSRO, P2SRO (McAleer, Lanier, Fox, Baldi; NeurIPS 2020).** P2SRO finds that DCH and Rectified PSRO "fail to converge even in small games". It proves that Rectified PSRO with an exact best response does not converge to a Nash equilibrium in all symmetric zero-sum normal-form games (Prop. 3.1), and finds that DCH cannot get low exploitability on Leduc poker. P2SRO keeps a hierarchy of active workers, each training against the frozen and lower-level policies, with convergence guarantees and speed-up that grows with the number of workers. It notes that in large games "basic skills ... may need to be relearned when starting each iteration from scratch". — [McAleer et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.08555)
- P2SRO on Barrage Stratego (game-tree complexity ~10^50): a 71% average win rate against existing bots after 820,000 episodes (Asmodeus 81%, Celsius 70%, Vixen 69%, Celsius1.1 65%). It trains one population for both seats of a non-symmetric game, because that is easier than training two. — [McAleer et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.08555)
- **Diverse PSRO / DPP (Perez-Nieves, Yang, Slumbers, Mguni, Wen, Wang; ICML 2021).** Covered under the diversity question below. Against PSRO, PSRO_rN, P2SRO and α-PSRO it achieves "at least the same, and in most games, lower exploitability". — [Perez-Nieves et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.07927)
- **Nash averaging (Balduzzi, Tuyls, Pérolat, Graepel; NeurIPS 2018)** is an evaluation method that "automatically adapts to redundancies in evaluation data", so results are not biased by easy tasks or weak agents. The authors argue for "maximally inclusive evaluation" (adding agents does no harm beyond compute). (Abstract only.) — [Balduzzi et al. 2018](https://arxiv.org/abs/1806.02643)
- **α-Rank (Omidshafiei, Papadimitriou, Piliouras, Tuyls, Rowland, Lespiau, Czarnecki, Lanctot, Pérolat, Munos; 2019)** ranks agents with evolutionary dynamics on the empirical game. Its solution concept is the Markov-Conley chain, which captures cycles and recurrent sets rather than a static fixed point. It runs in polynomial time in the number of pure strategy profiles, whereas Nash is intractable in general-sum games. Validated on AlphaGo, AlphaZero, MuJoCo Soccer and poker. (Abstract only.) — [Omidshafiei et al. 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1903.01373)
- Project history: metagame-rl already ran a PSRO deck builder with a Nash meta-solver over a real-game matrix (`rl/psro.py`, `rl/nash.py`; removed at log #65, recoverable at git tag `pre-simplify`). One run (population 64) ended with "final equilibrium a single deck, `psro11-1` (Wigglytuff-like)". — [progress-log.md](C:/Users/evanc/Desktop/333/metagame-rl/notes/progress-log.md) (≈line 518); [README.md](C:/Users/evanc/Desktop/333/metagame-rl/README.md) (line 294)

### Inferences
- (Inference) The current loop, in which each deck moves to an edit that scores better against the population, is closest to PSRO with a *uniform* meta-strategy over the current population (fictitious-play-like, max-average). Lanctot's "sharpening" failure and AlphaStar's warning that pursuing the mean win rate gives exploitable policies (next section) both predict F1: once walls are the plurality, the average opponent is a wall, and wall-mirror improvements dominate the gradient.
- (Inference) A Nash meta-solver alone will not fix F1 here, for two reasons. The project's own PSRO run already collapsed to a single-deck equilibrium. And Balduzzi shows Nash-based PSRO degenerates to self-play when one agent dominates the payoff matrix. If the matrix is computed with the co-adapted pilot (F2), walls *are* dominant in that matrix, so Nash will correctly but uselessly concentrate on them. The solver has to be paired with a payoff matrix that the shared pilot has not co-adapted to (see the co-adaptation question).
- (Inference) Cost for metagame-rl: the oracle is a deck edit plus a few hundred games, not an RL run. So the solver costs (Nash LP, α-Rank, DPP on a ≤99×99 matrix) are negligible next to game simulation. The binding cost is the O(n²) matrix: 99 decks means 4,851 pairs. Clustering decks into types (or using the existing type labels) and estimating a type × type matrix cuts this by orders of magnitude.

### Gaps
- No exact compute numbers (GPU-hours) for PSRO_rN or DPP-PSRO in deep-RL domains were found. Those papers measure cost in oracle calls or iterations.
- Nash averaging and α-Rank were read from abstracts only. Their exact claims about Elo's sensitivity to redundant agents were not extracted.
- Venues for Balduzzi 2019 (ICML), Czarnecki 2020 (NeurIPS) and Pipeline PSRO (NeurIPS) are from the publication record, not from the fetched arXiv PDFs (which carry no venue line). The α-Rank arXiv page lists no journal (it was published in Scientific Reports 2019; not verified here).

## AlphaStar league (main agents, main exploiters, league exploiters, PFSP): why each role exists, what failed without them, results of ablations

### Takeaway
AlphaStar's league exists because plain self-play "chases cycles" and forgets, and uniform fictitious self-play wastes games on beaten opponents. Each extra role measurably raised both main-agent strength and league robustness in ablations: Elo 1540 → 1693 → 1824 and relative population performance 6% → 35% → 62% as main exploiters and then league exploiters were added. But the whole system leaned heavily on human data: without it, test Elo was 149 against 1540. Its compute (12 learning agents × 32 TPUv3 × 44 days) is far outside a desktop budget, so what transfers is the matchmaking rules, not the scale.

### Cited Findings
- Self-play "may chase cycles (for example, where A defeats B, and B defeats C, but A loses to C) indefinitely without making progress". Fictitious self-play (FSP) avoids cycles by best-responding to a uniform mixture of all previous policies, but "many games are wasted against players that are defeated in almost 100% of games". — [Vinyals et al., Nature 2019 (preprint PDF)](https://storage.googleapis.com/deepmind-media/research/alphastar/AlphaStar_unformatted.pdf); [Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-019-1724-z)
- **PFSP.** An opponent B is sampled with probability proportional to f(P[A beats B]). f_hard(x) = (1−x)^p focuses on the hardest opponents ("no games are played against opponents that the agent already beats"). This is a smooth approximation of max-min rather than FSP's max-average, and it matters "in highly non-transitive games like StarCraft, where the pursuit of the mean win-rate might lead to policies that are easy to exploit". It also integrates rare, strong exploits that a uniform mixture "would be able to just ignore". f_var(x) = x(1−x) plays opponents near the agent's own level, and is used for main exploiters and struggling main agents. — [Vinyals et al. 2019](https://storage.googleapis.com/deepmind-media/research/alphastar/AlphaStar_unformatted.pdf)
- **Main agents** train with 35% self-play, 50% PFSP against all past league players, and 15% PFSP against "forgotten" main players they can no longer beat and past main exploiters (falling back to self-play if there are none). A frozen copy is added every 2×10^9 steps, and main agents never reset. — [Vinyals et al. 2019](https://storage.googleapis.com/deepmind-media/research/alphastar/AlphaStar_unformatted.pdf)
- **Main exploiters** play only the current main agents. Their purpose is to "identify potential exploits in the main agents", so the main agents are "encouraged to address their weaknesses". When their win rate is below 20%, half the time they use PFSP-var over earlier main-agent snapshots, which "forms a curriculum which facilitates learning". They are added to the league when they beat all three main agents in more than 70% of games, or after a timeout of 4×10^9 steps, and are then reset to the supervised parameters. — [Vinyals et al. 2019](https://storage.googleapis.com/deepmind-media/research/alphastar/AlphaStar_unformatted.pdf)
- **League exploiters** train with PFSP against the whole league and "are not targeted by main exploiter agents". Their purpose is "to find systemic weaknesses of the entire league" ("global blind spots ... strategies that no player in the League can beat, but that are not necessarily robust themselves"). They are added at >70% against all league players or after a timeout of 2×10^9 steps, then reset with 25% probability. Both exploiter types are "periodically reinitialized to encourage more diversity". — [Vinyals et al. 2019](https://storage.googleapis.com/deepmind-media/research/alphastar/AlphaStar_unformatted.pdf)
- **League-composition ablation** (Fig. 3A-B; simplified setup: one map, Protoss v Protoss, 10^10 steps, otherwise 50/50 SP/PFSP). Main-agent test Elo was 1540 with main agents only, 1693 with main exploiters added, and 1824 with league exploiters added. Relative population performance of the whole league, "which measures exploitability", was 6%, 35% and 62% respectively. — [Vinyals et al. 2019](https://storage.googleapis.com/deepmind-media/research/alphastar/AlphaStar_unformatted.pdf)
- **Matchmaking ablation** (Fig. 3C-D). Test Elo: PFSP+SP 1540, SP 1519, PFSP 1273, FSP 1143. The forgetting proxy, the minimum win rate against all past versions averaged over time, is 71% for PFSP+SP and 46% for SP, with PFSP and FSP at 69-70%. (The text-extracted figure layout makes the 69/70 assignment between PFSP and FSP ambiguous; the SP value is unambiguous.) Caption: "Naive self-play has high Elo, but is more forgetful." — [Vinyals et al. 2019](https://storage.googleapis.com/deepmind-media/research/alphastar/AlphaStar_unformatted.pdf)
- **Human-data ablation** (Fig. 3E): the full system scored a test Elo of 1540 and "No human data" scored 149 (intermediate variants roughly 936-1400). In the full league, exploiters are reset to supervised (human-imitation) parameters. — [Vinyals et al. 2019](https://storage.googleapis.com/deepmind-media/research/alphastar/AlphaStar_unformatted.pdf)
- During the full run, the main agents "performed better against the held-out validation agents" and the main exploiters' performance "actually reduced over time", both read as growing robustness. The league Nash put small weight on old players, "suggesting that the learning algorithm does not cycle or regress". "Unlike the main agents, the exploiters rapidly explore different unit compositions." — [Vinyals et al. 2019](https://storage.googleapis.com/deepmind-media/research/alphastar/AlphaStar_unformatted.pdf)
- Compute: three main agents, three main exploiters and six league exploiters (12 learners), each trained on 32 TPUv3 for 44 days, with 16,000 concurrent matches per training agent. "Almost 900 distinct players were created." — [Vinyals et al. 2019](https://storage.googleapis.com/deepmind-media/research/alphastar/AlphaStar_unformatted.pdf)
- **OpenAI Five** used a much cheaper mix: 80% of games against the current self and 20% against past selves, explicitly to avoid "strategy collapse". — [OpenAI, "OpenAI Five" (2018)](https://openai.com/index/openai-five)
- **Minimax Exploiter (Bairamian, Marcotte, Romoff, Robert, Nowrouzezahrai; AAMAS 2024)** targets the cost of league exploiters, which find counter-strategies more slowly as training goes on. The exploiter's reward adds the frozen main agent's value at the next state: R − αγ(1−d)·max_a Q_main(s′,a). This turns a sparse win/loss into a dense signal. In For Honor over 24 h, it reached 11 converged exploiter generations per seed against at most 9 for other exploiter types, and 16 against 13 for the vanilla exploiter over 100 h, with convergence defined as 85% against the frozen main agent. The resulting main agent was "the most robust", beating the other main agents 66.25-80.26%. — [Bairamian et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.17190)
- Project history: exploiter draft heads were built (`--draft-exploit`, log #123). Fresh exploiters "lose almost every game" and get almost no signal. Exploiters started as copies of the main head begin near 0.5, but both fresh and copied heads drifted to the main type (#125). In one exploiter card, the main head's decks went to 38 Energy. A further exploiter league from the 479 snapshot was started at #143. — [progress-log.md](C:/Users/evanc/Desktop/333/metagame-rl/notes/progress-log.md) (#123, #125, #143)

### Inferences
- (Inference, F3) Two missing AlphaStar mechanics match the project's exploiter failures exactly. (a) Fresh exploiters with no signal is what AlphaStar's f_var curriculum over *older* main snapshots, used when the win rate is below 20%, is for. (b) Exploiters drifting to the main type is what the 70%-threshold-then-reset rule prevents: an exploiter is frozen into the league as soon as it is a genuine counter, then reset, so it never stays long enough to settle into "a slightly better copy". In AlphaStar the reset target is the human-imitation policy. Here the closest self-discovery equivalent is a random deck or a fresh head, which gives no signal, so the f_var curriculum is what makes resets workable.
- (Inference, F1) The project's selection uses the mean result against the population. AlphaStar's PFSP-hard argument (max-min instead of max-average) bears directly on it: a deck scored against its hardest opponents in the population is not rewarded for farming the wall mirror.
- (Inference, F5) The 149 against 1540 Elo human-data ablation is the clearest published evidence that league machinery alone did not solve exploration from scratch in a complex game. The project's no-human-data constraint puts it in the regime AlphaStar did not show working.
- (Inference, compute) The cheapest transferable piece is a matchmaking rule, either OpenAI Five's 80/20 or AlphaStar's 35/50/15. Neither adds learners. Exploiters add learners, but in metagame-rl a draft head is small; the binding cost is the games each head's decks consume.

- Extended Data Fig. 5 (the in-depth SP/FSP/PFSP comparison) was not read. Ablation numbers come from text extracted from the main Fig. 3, so the layout introduces some ambiguity (noted above).
- No published ablation was found of AlphaStar-style leagues at desktop scale. TStarBot-X and similar reduced-compute replications were not checked.

## Non-transitivity and "spinning tops" (Balduzzi et al. 2019, Czarnecki et al. 2020): implications for training populations and for measuring progress

### Takeaway
Real games have a large transitive ("skill") axis, plus non-transitive cycles that are widest at intermediate skill. Training only makes reliable transitive progress if the population covers a whole Nash cluster at its current skill level. Below a critical population size, training cycles in every real game tested; above it, training converges. Progress has to be measured on populations, with Nash-based metrics such as relative population performance (RPP), not by win rate against the training population.

### Cited Findings
- Any zero-sum functional-form game decomposes into a transitive component and a cyclic component. Self-play suits transitive games, but in non-transitive games "self-play cycles through agents without improving". — [Balduzzi et al. 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1901.08106)
- **Effective diversity**, d(P) = pᵀ·⌊A_P⌋₊·p with p the Nash of the population's evaluation matrix, measures "how the best agents (those with support in the maximum entropy Nash) exploit each other. If there is a dominant agent then diversity is zero." **Relative population performance** compares populations through the Nash of the cross-population payoff matrix. — [Balduzzi et al. 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1901.08106)
- "Why ignore weaknesses?": training each Nash-supported agent against the agents it *loses* to points the gradients inward and contracts the gamescape. "Training rock against paper would make it more like scissors ... building objectives using the weak points of agents does not encourage diverse niches." — [Balduzzi et al. 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1901.08106)
- **Spinning tops (Czarnecki, Gidel, Tracey, Tuyls, Omidshafiei, Balduzzi, Jaderberg; 2020).** Games of Skill have a geometry like a spinning top: the upright axis is transitive strength, and the radial axis is the number of cycles at that strength. In Go (3×3), Nash clusters are "maximal around the mid-ranges of transitive strength" and shrink toward the top and bottom, but "at the level of the strongest strategies, non-trivial Nash clusters exist". The analysis used about 1,000 sampled agents per game, from alpha-beta and MCTS at varied strength and seeds. — [Czarnecki et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2004.09468)
- Prop. 3: fixed-memory fictitious play, which replaces a population member with one that beats the population on average, converges in layered Games of Skill if the population is no smaller than the layer it occupies. "To guarantee transitive improvements over time, it is important to cover all possible game styles." Theorem 3: if the training population contains a full Nash cluster, training against it guarantees transitive improvement. — [Czarnecki et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2004.09468)
- Empirically, the oldest member is replaced by the *weakest* strategy that beats the population on average (a pessimistic oracle chosen to mimic greedy RL). "For small population sizes, training does not converge and cycles for all games. As the population grows, strength increases but saturates in various suboptimal cycles. However, when the population exceeds a critical size, training converges to the best strategies in almost all experiments." — [Czarnecki et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2004.09468)
- Priors (search, architecture, imitation) "initialise in higher parts of the spinning top", where less non-transitivity handling is needed. The authors describe "a form of balance between priors one builds into an AI system and the amount of required multi-agent learning complexity". — [Czarnecki et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2004.09468)
- In non-transitive games the strength of an individual strategy is ill-defined, so evaluation should compare populations via RPP and Nash. — [Balduzzi et al. 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1901.08106); [NeuPL, Liu et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2202.07415)

### Inferences
- (Inference, F1) A 99-deck population ought to be large enough to cover a Nash cluster of deck types, but a population that has collapsed to walls has a *covered* size of about 1 type. In spinning-top terms it then behaves like the "small population" regime that cycles or stalls. So the effective population size (distinct types under the Nash, or effective diversity) is the quantity to protect, not the deck count. A type cap enforces this crudely; it matches the project's finding that a type cap and frequent turnover keep the pilot general.
- (Inference, F3) Balduzzi's "why ignore weaknesses" result is a warning for any rule that trains a deck or head against what currently beats it. That is the natural design for anti-wall exploiters, and it pulls them toward the opponent's type (contraction). This is consistent with exploiter heads drifting to the main type.
- (Inference, measuring progress) Within-population mean result is not a progress metric. The project should log, per checkpoint: (i) the maximum-entropy Nash of the real-game matrix over deck types and its support size; (ii) effective diversity; (iii) RPP of the current population against frozen past populations and against a fixed evaluation pool. The human lists may be used *for evaluation only*, consistent with the no-human-data training rule.
- (Inference, F5) Czarnecki's priors argument suggests why from-scratch runs collapse into deck-out Pokémon piles: with a weak pilot the population sits low on the top, where a degenerate strategy can be transitively "best" against what exists. Allowed priors, such as general-purpose search at play time, would start the population higher.

- No paper found that measures spinning-top geometry for collectible card games or deck-building meta-games specifically.
- The critical population sizes per game in Czarnecki et al. are shown in plots (Table 1 figures) and were not extracted numerically.

## Policy-space and behavioural diversity regularisers (DPP diversity, Perez-Nieves et al. 2021, unified diversity measures): evidence they prevent collapse

### Takeaway
Diversity defined on *payoffs* (response diversity) is cheap to compute from a matchup matrix and comes with gamescape-enlargement guarantees. Diversity defined on *behaviour* (occupancy measures, trajectories) needs rollouts and embeddings. Evidence that these regularisers prevent collapse is strong in normal-form games and meta-games (including the 888-policy AlphaStar meta-game), but thin in deep-RL domains at scale.

### Cited Findings
- **DPP diversity (Perez-Nieves et al., ICML 2021).** The diversity of a population S is the expected cardinality of a determinantal point process whose kernel is the Gram matrix of the payoff rows (L = M·Mᵀ): Diversity(S) = Tr(I − (L + I)^−1), computable in O(|S|³). It differs from matrix rank and from effective diversity: duplicates add little, and near-duplicates add little. — [Perez-Nieves et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.07927)
- Diverse best response: argmax over π of [payoff(π, opponent mixture) + τ·Diversity(S ∪ {π})], where τ is tunable. Adding such a response provably "strictly enlarges the gamescape", without PSRO_rN's assumption that opponents play Nash (Prop. 9). They also give diverse FP and diverse α-PSRO (quality-diversity DPP kernel) with convergence results. — [Perez-Nieves et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.07927)
- Results: tested on "tens of games" with strong non-transitivity, including meta-games from the 28 real-world games of Czarnecki et al. On the AlphaStar meta-game (888 × 888) it gets the lowest exploitability "by only using less than 50 out of 888 RL policies", and "exploitability keeps approaching zero while other methods saturate". The authors' explanation: "without accounting for behavioural diversity, PSRO baselines tend to enter into a cyclic phase where repetitive strategies already in the population are found". On a 7-Gaussian non-transitive mixture model, PSRO and PSRO_rN "fail to complete the task" while DPP-PSRO reaches near-zero exploitability. — [Perez-Nieves et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.07927)
- **Behavioural and response diversity (Liu, Jia, Wen, Yang, Hu, Chen, Fan, Hu; 2021).** Defines behavioural diversity as discrepancies between occupancy measures (state-action distributions), and response diversity as differences in a policy's payoffs against different opponents. It shows that earlier measures cover one or the other but not both. It also argues exploitability is a limited evaluation metric and proposes "population effectivity". Results: lowest exploitability and highest population effectivity on a matrix game and the non-transitive mixture model, and the largest goal difference against opponents of various levels in Google Research Football. — [Liu et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2106.04958)
- Project history: notes/analysis-2026-10-06 already recommends "response diversity with an adaptive weight": score candidates by win rate against the mixture plus λ·DPP gain, with λ adapted online as in DvD (Parker-Holder et al., NeurIPS 2020), and report α-Rank next to Nash. Its simplest first version (decided 2026-10-07) is a k-nearest-neighbour novelty bonus on the pilot's start-of-game embedding. A grep of the progress log did not show either being run. — [analysis-2026-10-06.md](C:/Users/evanc/Desktop/333/metagame-rl/notes/analysis-2026-10-06.md) (items "Diversity without losing strength", next step 4)

### Inferences
- (Inference, F1, F3) The DPP term on payoff rows needs nothing beyond the deck × deck (or type × type) result matrix the loop already plays. So adding τ·ΔDiversity to the acceptance score for a deck edit costs microseconds. Because it is defined on *responses*, it rewards a deck that beats different things, not one that merely looks different. That is the right lever against walls: a deck that beats walls but loses to other decks has a new payoff row and gets credit, while a fourth wall variant does not.
- (Inference) The DPP bonus also gives the draft heads a non-degenerate target that does not depend on their own entropy. That bears on F3 (entropy collapse 3 → 0.5 nats), where an entropy bonus alone did not restore Trainers (#137 notes).
- (Inference, caveat) DPP diversity is only as good as the payoff matrix. If the matrix is computed with a pilot co-adapted to walls (F2), the payoff rows reflect pilot skill per type, not deck quality per type. So the diversity term should use a matrix from a pilot that is general across types (see next section).
- (Inference) Behavioural (occupancy) diversity is a poorer fit for decks than response diversity: two decks with different play patterns can have identical matchups. It may suit the *pilot's* play styles better.

### Gaps
- No deep-RL evidence was found at the scale of a full game (StarCraft- or Dota-class) that a DPP term prevents collapse during training. The published evidence is on meta-games built from pre-trained policies, small games and Google Research Football.
- The Liu et al. 2021 venue (NeurIPS 2021 per publication record) was not verified in the fetched text. Its exact numbers were not extracted.
- "A unified diversity measure" follow-ups (e.g. Yao et al., NeurIPS 2023, policy-space diversity PSRO, mentioned in the project notes) were not read here.

## Co-adaptation/overfitting of policies to their training partners (Lanctot 2017 JPC metric, cross-play): measurements and remedies

### Takeaway
Policies trained together overfit to each other. Lanctot's joint-policy correlation (JPC) measures this as the loss of return when a policy meets an independently trained partner: up to 71.7% in their gridworlds. Training against mixtures of past policies (PSRO/DCH) cut it by 29-57 percentage points. F2 (walls 0.72-0.76 against the human lists with their own pilot, 0.23-0.24 with an older generalist pilot) is a JPC-type effect between a *shared pilot* and the *deck population* it is trained on.

### Cited Findings
- **JPC (Lanctot et al. 2017).** Train D seeds of the same setup and build a D × D matrix of returns when player 1 is taken from seed i and player 2 from seed j. Diagonals are policies trained together; off-diagonals are policies trained apart. The average proportional loss is R̄ = (D̄ − Ō)/D̄, where D̄ and Ō are the mean diagonal and off-diagonal values. — [Lanctot et al. 2017](https://arxiv.org/abs/1711.00832)
- JPC numbers, independent RL against DCH (Reactor, 2 players, 10 levels), on Laser Tag:
  - small2: D̄ 30.44, Ō 20.03, R̄ 0.342, against DCH 0.055
  - small3: R̄ 0.625 against 0.082
  - small4: R̄ 0.717 against 0.150
  - The reductions are 28.7, 54.3 and 56.7 percentage points.
  - With only the top-level policy instead of the full meta-strategy: R̄ = 0.147, 0.27, 0.118.
  - On small4, level 5 cuts JPC almost as much as level 10 (56.1 against 56.7 points); level 3 cuts it by 44 points.
  - Gathering and Pathfind show no JPC problem, "presumably because coordination is not required".
  - The authors: "an independently-learned policy could expect to lose 34.2% of its reward when playing with another independently-learned policy even though it was trained under identical circumstances!"
  — [Lanctot et al. 2017](https://arxiv.org/abs/1711.00832)
- AlphaStar measured robustness against *held-out* validation agents and through the declining success of main exploiters, not through the training league's own win rates. — [Vinyals et al. 2019](https://storage.googleapis.com/deepmind-media/research/alphastar/AlphaStar_unformatted.pdf)
- **NeuPL (Liu, Marris, Hennes, Merel, Heess, Graepel; ICLR 2022)** represents the whole population in a single conditional network π(·|s, σ_i), where σ_i is the opponent mixture policy i trains against. This allows transfer across policies and avoids two problems of iterative PSRO: truncated, under-trained best responses, and relearning basic skills each iteration. "Novel strategies become more accessible, not less, as the neural population expands." The number of distinct policies is set by the meta-graph solver, not by hand-made truncation rules. — [Liu et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2202.07415)
- NeuPL needs the initial "sink" strategy to be exploitable: "had the sink strategy been initialized to be the Nash mixture strategy, subsequent strategies would be 'uninteresting' as no strategy can improve upon the sink strategy." — [Liu et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2202.07415)
- Project history: a design workflow at #138 "dropped a PFSP pool of past pilots". The earlier pilot-v2 was trained with a league of past snapshots. The 2026-10-06 analysis suggested NeuPL-style conditioning on a snapshot index to fix the league's batching slowdown (collection grew from 7 s to 55 s an iteration). — [progress-log.md](C:/Users/evanc/Desktop/333/metagame-rl/notes/progress-log.md) (#138, ≈line 1620); [analysis-2026-10-06.md](C:/Users/evanc/Desktop/333/metagame-rl/notes/analysis-2026-10-06.md)

### Inferences
- (Inference, F2) Mapping JPC onto the project: the "partners" are (pilot, deck population) pairs from different runs or iterations. A cross-play matrix with rows = deck populations (run A, run B, a frozen archive of past populations, the human lists for evaluation only) and columns = pilots (own, generalist pilot-v2, other runs' final pilots) gives a direct R̄ per run. The observed 0.74 → 0.235 drop for walls corresponds to R̄ ≈ 0.68 by Lanctot's formula, comparable to the worst Laser Tag map. This metric can be a stop/go signal under the project's "kill stalled runs early" rule.
- (Inference, F2 → F1) The causal chain the literature suggests: the pilot trains mostly on the population's majority type, so it plays that type (and its mirror) best. The payoff matrix then overrates that type. Deck selection on that matrix reinforces the type, and the loop closes. Lanctot's remedy is to train against a *mixture over past and independent partners*. For the pilot that means a fixed share of its games on decks from a frozen archive of past populations and random decks: 20% as in OpenAI Five, or a PFSP-hard share over deck types where its value error or loss rate is worst. The KL-to-generalist anchor (+65 Elo on the human lists but decks unchanged) acts on the policy, not on the data distribution. The literature points at the data distribution.
- (Inference, F2 → selection) Score deck edits with a pilot that did not co-adapt to the current population. Options: (a) a frozen generalist pilot (the 0.23 against 0.74 gap shows how much this changes the verdict); (b) the average over 2-3 pilots from different runs or snapshots, a cheap "ensemble of partners" in Lanctot's sense; (c) a NeuPL-style pilot conditioned on an opponent-mixture code, so the selection pilot is the one trained against the Nash mixture rather than against the raw population.
- (Inference) Lanctot's level-5 against level-10 result (56.1 against 56.7 points) suggests a small number of distinct training partners already captures most of the de-correlation benefit. Two or three frozen pilot snapshots may be enough, which is compute-frugal.

### Gaps
- No paper was found on co-adaptation between a *shared actor* and the *distribution of environments* (decks) it is trained on, as opposed to between two learning agents. The mapping above is by analogy. The closest literature (unsupervised environment design, PLR/ACCEL) is outside this assignment's scope.
- Cross-play literature from cooperative MARL (e.g. Other-Play, Fictitious Co-Play) was not read; it concerns coordination, not zero-sum play.

## Which of these map onto F1-F3 and F6, and what a compute-frugal version would look like

### Takeaway
F1-F3 look like one coupled failure: a uniform, max-average selection objective, plus a payoff matrix produced by a pilot co-adapted to the majority type, plus no mechanism that values *different* responses. A compute-frugal version keeps the existing loop and changes three cheap things. (1) What the pilot trains on: a past-population share and PFSP over types. (2) Which matrix and objective decks are selected on: a de-correlated pilot, a Nash or PFSP-hard objective, and a DPP response-diversity bonus. (3) How exploiters are run: an f_var curriculum, a 70% freeze threshold, then a reset. F6 has no direct support in this literature.

### Cited Findings
- Max-average opponents produce exploitable policies in non-transitive games, and PFSP-hard approximates max-min. — [Vinyals et al. 2019](https://storage.googleapis.com/deepmind-media/research/alphastar/AlphaStar_unformatted.pdf)
- Uniform fictitious play "sharpens" onto repeated best responses; full-support γ-exploratory meta-strategies are Lanctot's fix. — [Lanctot et al. 2017](https://arxiv.org/abs/1711.00832)
- Payoff-row DPP diversity costs O(|S|³) and provably enlarges the gamescape. — [Perez-Nieves et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.07927)
- Covering a full Nash cluster guarantees transitive progress; populations below a critical size cycle. — [Czarnecki et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2004.09468)
- Exploiters need a curriculum when weak (f_var below 20% win rate), freezing at >70%, and resets. — [Vinyals et al. 2019](https://storage.googleapis.com/deepmind-media/research/alphastar/AlphaStar_unformatted.pdf)
- A dense value-based exploiter reward (Minimax Exploiter) produced converged exploiters faster in a commercial game. — [Bairamian et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.17190)
- 80/20 current/past matchmaking was used to avoid strategy collapse in Dota 2. — [OpenAI 2018](https://openai.com/index/openai-five)
- Training against weaknesses contracts the gamescape; rectified Nash grows niches but can over-specialise and has no convergence guarantee. — [Balduzzi et al. 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1901.08106); [McAleer et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.08555)

### Inferences
A compute-frugal plan, ordered by cost. Everything below is (Inference); costs are rough estimates for one RTX 5070 Ti.

1. **Measure before changing (F2, F3; ~free beyond eval games).** Every K iterations, log:
   - the cross-play/JPC table (populations × pilots, R̄ per run);
   - the maximum-entropy Nash support size over deck types on the real-game matrix;
   - effective diversity (Balduzzi) and DPP expected cardinality;
   - RPP against frozen past populations.
   Kill a run whose R̄ rises while Nash support shrinks.
2. **Pilot data distribution (F2, and indirectly F1; ~0 extra compute, it reallocates games).**
   - Take a fixed share of pilot games (start at 20%, after OpenAI Five) from a frozen archive of past-population decks plus random decks.
   - Sample the remaining population games by PFSP-hard over deck *types*, weighted toward types where the pilot's value error or loss rate is highest, so the majority type stops dominating the pilot's data.
   - This replaces the KL anchor's policy-space regularisation with data-space coverage. Prior attempt: a "PFSP pool of past pilots" was dropped at #138. That was about opponent *pilots*, not opponent *decks*, so this is a different intervention.
3. **De-correlated selection matrix (F1, F2, F3; cost: the selection games played by 2 pilots instead of 1).** Score candidate edits by the mean over {current pilot, a frozen generalist snapshot}, or by the generalist alone. The project's own number (walls 0.74 against 0.235) shows the verdict depends on this choice.
4. **Selection objective (F1, F3; negligible compute).** Replace "better than own version on average against the population" with:
   - win rate against the max-entropy Nash mixture over types (or PFSP-hard weights), plus τ·ΔDPP diversity on payoff rows, with τ adapted as in DvD (already planned in analysis-2026-10-06 step 4 and apparently not yet run);
   - keep γ-exploratory full support (Lanctot), so no type's weight goes to zero.
   - Caveat: the earlier Nash-PSRO collapsed to one deck. Step 3 has to come with this one.
5. **Exploiter heads, AlphaStar rules (F3; cost: as now).**
   - Main exploiters: judged against the main head's decks. When their win rate is below 20%, half their games use f_var over *older* main-head snapshots (fixes "fresh exploiters get no signal").
   - Freeze an exploiter's decks into the population once they beat the main head's decks more than 70%, or at a timeout. Then reset the head (fixes drifting to the main type).
   - League exploiters: judged against the whole population with PFSP-hard, reset with 25% probability.
   - Optional: a Minimax-Exploiter-style dense score (the main pilot's value of the exploiter's opponent position at each step), which cuts the noise of win/loss-only selection.
6. **Rectified-Nash niches (F1; negligible compute, higher risk).** Improve each Nash-supported type only against decks it beats or ties (PSRO_rN), so anti-wall decks are not trained toward walls (Balduzzi's contraction result). Risk: no convergence guarantee (McAleer) and over-specialised niches.
7. **NeuPL-style pilot (F2, F4; engineering cost, then normal training).** Condition the shared pilot on a small opponent-mixture code (e.g. a Nash-mixture id over types), so one network holds a population of responses with transfer between them. It is the frugal alternative to a full league of pilots. The project already noted this to fix the league batching slowdown.

Mapping for F6 (turn limit, draws): no source in this set addresses it. (Inference) Scoring unfinished games as draws pulls payoff entries toward 0.5. That shrinks the rectified payoffs that effective diversity, DPP and Nash support all depend on, so it would *weaken* the diversity mechanisms above rather than help them. This is consistent with F6's null result.

Mapping for F4 and F5 (outside the main scope): AlphaStar's 149 against 1540 Elo human-data ablation, and Czarnecki's priors-move-you-up-the-top argument, are the population-literature reasons to expect from-scratch collapse (F5) without strong priors. General-purpose search (allowed under the self-discovery rule) is the kind of prior those papers credit. NeuPL's note that an initial strategy at Nash yields "uninteresting" successors cautions against starting the population from a single strong deck type.

### Gaps
- None of the cited methods has been tested on a two-level problem in which a shared policy plays (pilot) while a population chooses environments (decks). The mapping is analogical.
- Selection at the noise floor (73% of decks moving with no better candidate) is a statistical problem. The relevant literature (e.g. α-Rank under incomplete information, Rowland et al., NeurIPS 2019; bandit-based empirical game estimation) was not read in this assignment.
