Source status: read in full (text extracted from the PDF or LaTeX source): Kakade & Langford 2002 (LaTeX source), Agarwal et al. 2019/2021, Tavakoli et al. 2018/2020, Backplay, Florensa et al. 2017, Go-Explore 2019 and Nature 2021, Salimans & Chen 2018, TRPO (vine section), VinePPO, ReBeL (sections 1-5), SPARTA (method), LBS (method and results), αµ (intro), PLR and Robust PLR (method). ## 1. Kakade & Langford 2002 (CPI, restart distribution mu): what the theory says and what the guarantee depends on

### Takeaway
CPI optimises η_μ(π) = E_{s~μ}[V_π(s)] under an exploratory restart distribution μ instead of the start distribution D. An optimal policy maximises both objectives, but inside a restricted policy class the η_μ-maximiser can be poor under η_D. The guarantee on η_D scales with the mismatch ‖d_{π*,D}/d_{π,μ}‖∞ ≤ ‖d_{π*,D}/μ‖∞/(1-γ), so a μ that puts mass on the states the optimal policy visits (here, the rare decision states) tightens the bound. The number of iterations does not depend on μ. Agarwal et al. later showed that the same mismatch coefficient controls policy-gradient convergence, and that without it gradients can vanish exponentially.

### Cited Findings
- Definition: "A μ restart distribution draws the next state from the distribution μ." This is weaker than a generative model, but "a much stronger assumption than having only 'irreversible' experience". If μ is relatively uniform (not necessarily D), it removes the need for explicit exploration. — [Kakade & Langford 2002, LaTeX source](https://hunch.net/~jl/projects/aoarl/Final.tex_back) ([PDF](https://people.eecs.berkeley.edu/~pabbeel/cs287-fa09/readings/KakadeLangford-icml2002.pdf); the PDF text did not extract, so the source was read)
- Objective: η_μ(π) ≡ E_{s~μ}[V_π(s)] with μ "some 'exploratory' restart distribution". Quote: "Any optimal policy simultaneously maximizes both η_μ and η_D. Unfortunately, the policy that maximizes η_μ within some restricted class of policies may have poor performance according to η_D." — [Kakade & Langford 2002](https://hunch.net/~jl/projects/aoarl/Final.tex_back)
- Why on-policy gradients fail: in a Thrun-style chain where random actions tend to move away from the goal, the expected time to the goal under a uniform policy is 3(2^n − n − 1), about 10^15 for n = 50. "Any sensible estimate of the gradient without reaching the goal state would be zero." Importance sampling is "not feasible" there because the weights "would have to be exponentially large". A small gradient estimate is accurate in magnitude but gives no direction. — [Kakade & Langford 2002](https://hunch.net/~jl/projects/aoarl/Final.tex_back)
- Conservative update: π_new = (1−α)π + απ′. The policy advantage is 𝔸_{π,μ}(π′) = E_{s~d_{π,μ}} E_{a~π′}[A_π(s,a)], and Δη_μ = (α/(1−γ))𝔸 + O(α²). Lower bound: η_μ(π_new) − η_μ(π) ≥ (α/(1−γ))(𝔸 − 2αγε/(1−γ(1−α))). With α = (1−γ)𝔸/(4R), the improvement is ≥ 𝔸²/(8R). — [Kakade & Langford 2002](https://hunch.net/~jl/projects/aoarl/Final.tex_back)
- Main theorem: with probability ≥ 1−δ, CPI (i) improves η_μ with every update, (ii) stops within 72R²/ε² calls to the ε-greedy policy chooser, and (iii) returns π with OPT(𝔸_{π,μ}) < 2ε. "The bound on the time until our algorithm ceases does not depend on the restart distribution μ though the performance of the policy π that we find does depend on μ." — [Kakade & Langford 2002](https://hunch.net/~jl/projects/aoarl/Final.tex_back)
- Performance guarantee (corollary): if OPT(𝔸_{π,μ}) < ε, then η_D(π*) − η_D(π) ≤ (ε/(1−γ))‖d_{π*,D}/d_{π,μ}‖∞ ≤ (ε/(1−γ)²)‖d_{π*,D}/μ‖∞. The second step uses d_{π,μ}(s) ≥ (1−γ)μ(s). "A more uniform d_{π,μ} ensures that the advantages are small at states that an optimal policy visits." — [Kakade & Langford 2002](https://hunch.net/~jl/projects/aoarl/Final.tex_back)
- Estimation: each iteration uses O((R²/ε²) log(R²/(δε²))) μ-restarts. A sample state comes from d_{π,μ} by starting at s₀~μ and accepting each state with probability (1−γ). The action is drawn uniformly and Q is estimated by rollout. The unbiased per-sample estimate is x_i = n_a Q̂(s,a)(π′(a;s) − π(a;s)), which is importance weighting over actions. — [Kakade & Langford 2002](https://hunch.net/~jl/projects/aoarl/Final.tex_back)
- Agarwal et al. define the distribution mismatch coefficient ‖d^{π*}_ρ/μ‖∞, where ρ is the performance measure and μ the optimisation (starting) distribution. Projected gradient ascent on V^π(μ) reaches ε-optimality for all ρ once T > 64|S||A|/((1−γ)⁶ε²)·‖d^{π*}_ρ/μ‖²∞. "Although the gradient is with respect to V^π(μ), the final guarantee applies to all distributions ρ … it may be helpful to optimize under the distribution μ." — [Agarwal, Kakade, Lee, Mahajan, "On the Theory of Policy Gradient Methods" (JMLR 2021)](https://arxiv.org/abs/1908.00261)
- Lower bound: on a chain MDP with H+2 states, policies with π(a₁|s) < 1/4 have gradients (and derivatives up to order ~H/(40 log 2H)) of norm ≤ (1/3)^{H/4}, yet are far from optimal. The mismatch is Ω(4^H) there and infinite when the start distribution is a single state. "The gradient can be small either due to π being near-optimal, or, simply because π does not visit advantageous states often enough." — [Agarwal et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1908.00261)
- Global convergence of softmax policy gradient assumes μ(s) > 0 for every s (Remark 5.1), because "d^π_μ(s) effectively scales down the learning rate for the parameters associated with state s". Tabular NPG with exact gradients converges with no dependence on the mismatch coefficient. — [Agarwal et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1908.00261)
- With function approximation, the NPG/Q-NPG bounds depend on a "transfer error" ε_bias: the fit error measured under the comparator's (optimal policy's) state distribution d*, not the fitting distribution. The alternative corollary uses ordinary approximation error "at the cost of also depending on the worst case distribution mismatch ratio". — [Agarwal et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1908.00261)
- CPI, PSDP and MD-MPI "give guarantees based on worst-case density ratios". The mismatch coefficient D is at most the concentrability coefficient C, and bounding D, unlike C, places no restriction on the MDP dynamics. — [Agarwal et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1908.00261)
- (Snippet only) A variance-reduced CPI paper describes the mismatch coefficient as measuring "how exploratory the restart distribution μ is". — [Variance-Reduced CPI, arXiv 2212.06283](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2212.06283)

### Inferences
- For the pilot, D is the self-play deal distribution, and d_{π,D} puts little mass on "Computer Search / Item Finder target pending" states (0.4–1.7 per game). CPI's bound and Agarwal's 1/μ(s) learning-rate effect both say these states learn slowly in proportion to their visitation. A μ that oversamples them raises d_{π,μ} exactly where it is small.
- Because d_{π,μ} is linear in μ, a mixture μ = (1−β)D + βR (R = restart pool) gives d_{π,μ} ≥ (1−β)d_{π,D}. On-policy states lose at most a factor (1−β) of weight, while the rare states gain β·d_{π,R}. This is my own derivation from the definition in Kakade & Langford, not a result stated in any paper.
- The theory guarantees nothing about bias from μ when the policy class contains the optimum. The risk comes entirely from limited capacity and shared parameters (see §3).

### Gaps
- Kakade & Langford's experiments section is absent from the source read; the paper is mainly theory. The 72R²/ε² bound is for an idealised ε-greedy policy chooser, not PPO.
- I did not find a paper that measures the mismatch coefficient, or the effect of μ, for a deep PPO agent in a game.

## 2. Backplay, reverse curriculum generation, Go-Explore and "own-state" restart variants: mechanisms, speed-ups, failure modes

### Takeaway
Every method in this family resets the simulator to states chosen to give a strong learning signal. The closest match to "restart from the agent's own visited states with PPO" is Tavakoli et al., *Exploring Restart Distributions*: a buffer of the agent's own states, 10% of transitions from restarts, uniform, TD-error-prioritised or return-prioritised. In a sparse-reward task the baseline failed to learn in 80% of runs, while the episodic-restart agent learned in all 42. Backplay and the backward algorithm need a trajectory, which can be the agent's own (as in Go-Explore's robustification). Reverse curriculum needs a goal state, which a card game lacks. Go-Explore needs determinism during exploration and a cell representation. The repeated failure modes are moving the start point too fast, a pool that collapses onto easy or narrow states, and poor coverage off the saved trajectory.

### Cited Findings
**Exploring Restart Distributions (Tavakoli, Levdik, Islam, Smith, Kormushev; RLDM 2019, arXiv v3 2020): agent's own states, PPO**
- Mechanism: a "restart memory" stores the agent's past (true) states. Initial states are drawn from a mixture of the environment start distribution p₁ and the buffer distribution p_B, keeping "a fixed ratio for the number of transitions that stem from augmented initial states … versus the total number of transitions". — [Tavakoli et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1811.11298)
- Three variants: uniform restart over recent states; prioritised restart with priority |δ_i|+ε for state-value TD error δ, sampled ∝ p_i^α; and episodic restart, which keeps the highest-return episodes, samples by return, then samples a state uniformly within the episode. — [Tavakoli et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1811.11298)
- Settings: ratio fixed at 0.1 ("10% of the total interactions stem from augmented initial states"), standard PPO hyperparameters, and restarted episodes truncated at T_aug = 10 (versus T_env = 1000) with bootstrapping at the cut. Memory size 20,000 and α = 0.4. FetchReach used 100 parent episodes with 10 sub-episodes each; Thrower used 50 with 10. — [Tavakoli et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1811.11298)
- Results: HalfCheetah showed "mild improvements" (5 seeds) and Humanoid "significant improvements" (curves only, no speed-up factor given). FetchReach improved mildly (10 seeds). On Thrower (sparse reward, 42 seeds), the episodic restart agent "learn[ed] consistently across all 42 independent runs (with each run achieving just under 50% success rate)", while "the baseline agent completely failed to learn any useful policy in 80% of the runs". Caveat: only runs that hit the goal within the first 50,000 steps were kept (about 10% of runs). — [Tavakoli et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1811.11298)
- Failure mode: with multi-goal tasks, episodic restart "could significantly hurt … by biasing its experiences towards specific parts of the state space". An easy-goal episode gets high priority and "quickly fill[s] the restart memory with episodes of a single goal". The fix is nested storage: each parent episode (from a true initial state) holds a limited number of sub-episodes, so the diversity of the true start distribution is kept. — [Tavakoli et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1811.11298)
- The TRPO vine procedure "can be realised as a special case" of restart distributions, and "restarting from previously-encountered states to sample more transitions reduces the variance of the gradient estimator". — [Tavakoli et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1811.11298)

**Backplay (Resnick, Raileanu, Kapoor, Peysakhovich, Cho, Bruna 2018)**
- Mechanism: from one demonstration (s₀…s_T), start each PPO episode at a state sampled uniformly from a window [T−k, T−j]. The window moves back on a hand-set epoch schedule until training starts only from s₀. Only states are needed, not actions. The demonstrator can be suboptimal. — [Backplay](https://arxiv.org/abs/1807.06919)
- Theory: in a simplified first-passage-time analysis (Markov chain with one absorbing goal), sampling starts uniformly along the demonstration is "slower by a factor of M" (the diameter) than Backplay's backward schedule. — [Backplay](https://arxiv.org/abs/1807.06919)
- Maze results (100 mazes, 2000 epochs; demonstrations optimal, 5 steps longer, 10 steps longer): % of mazes solved optimally: Standard 0; Uniform 27/51/49; Florensa 20/48/49; Backplay 31/37/54. % within 5 steps of optimal: Standard 0; Uniform 91/98/98; Florensa 51/77/69; Backplay 100/94/99. Mean extra steps: Backplay 0.64/7.94/0.37 against Florensa 63.36/25.44/39.75. "Standard does not learn a useful policy", and the agents beat their suboptimal demonstrators. — [Backplay](https://arxiv.org/abs/1807.06919)
- Pommerman (PPO, batch 102,400, 60 workers): Backplay learns where Standard and Uniform "fail to learn anything of note". It wins at least 50% on all 100 training maps and over 80% on more than half, and wins 416/1000 games on 10 held-out maps (18.3%–85.3% per map). Maze agents did not generalise to unseen mazes. — [Backplay](https://arxiv.org/abs/1807.06919)
- Practical notes: "Backplay does not perform well when the curriculum is advanced too quickly, however it does not fail when the curriculum is advanced 'too slowly'". Adaptive success-threshold schedules "worked but [were] too slow". The authors suspect the agent needs exposure "to enough states to attain a reasonable barometer of their value" rather than mastery. Success curves drop at each window advance. — [Backplay](https://arxiv.org/abs/1807.06919)
- Failure mode: "Backplay is not a universal strategy". With randomised s₀, one trajectory covers only "an exponentially small region around said trajectory". In a binary-tree example it gives no gain on average. — [Backplay](https://arxiv.org/abs/1807.06919)

**Reverse curriculum generation (Florensa, Held, Wulfmeier, Zhang, Abbeel 2017)**
- Assumptions: arbitrary resets, one goal state s_g given, and a uniform-random-action Markov chain whose communicating class contains the starts and the goal (reversibility). — [Florensa et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1707.05300)
- Mechanism: keep "good starts" whose estimated success lies in [R_min, R_max] = [0.1, 0.9]. New starts come from short "Brownian" random-action rollouts (T_B = 50, M = 10,000 candidates subsampled to N_new = 200) from good starts, mixed with N_old = 100 replayed old starts "to avoid catastrophic forgetting". Training uses TRPO with batch 50,000. Success is estimated from training rollouts rather than extra evaluation. — [Florensa et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1707.05300)
- Results: training directly on the target start distribution reached about 10% success on ring-on-peg and about 2% on key insertion (success only from near-goal starts), while the method reached the goal "from a wide range of far away start states". Ablation: dropping the good-start filter is still better than no curriculum but slower. An oracle that rejection-samples true good starts is better still but "orders of magnitude more expensive". — [Florensa et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1707.05300)
- Failure modes: asymmetric self-play as a start generator "gets stuck in a local optimum and fails to learn to reach the goal from more than 40% of start-states". Uniform-start TRPO "wrongly extrapolat[es] from these very few successful trajectories". The method only grows outward from one goal. — [Florensa et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1707.05300)
- In Backplay's maze comparison, Florensa-style agents had "higher sample complexity variance" and solved only 50–70% of maps. — [Backplay](https://arxiv.org/abs/1807.06919)

**Go-Explore (Ecoffet, Huizinga, Lehman, Stanley, Clune 2019; Nature 2021)**
- Mechanism: an archive of "cells" (downscaled frames, or domain-knowledge features) built from the agent's own visited states. Pick a promising cell, return to it by restoring the simulator state, explore with random actions (k = 100 frames, 95% chance of repeating the previous action), repeat. This addresses "detachment" and "derailment". Phase 2 then "robustifies" the best trajectories with the backward algorithm, run on the agent's own exploration trajectories. — [Go-Explore 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1901.10995); [First return, then explore (Nature 2021)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2004.12919)
- Numbers (2019, deterministic exploration phase, stochastic evaluation): Montezuma's Revenge mean 43,763 without domain knowledge and 666,474 with it. Pitfall mean 59,494 (max 107,363 of 112,000). Prior intrinsic-motivation methods averaged about 11,500 (max 17,500), and classic RL scored ≤ 2,500 "even with up to 22 billion game frames". — [Go-Explore 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1901.10995)
- Nature 2021 (sticky actions): robustified policies beat the state of the art and the average human on all 11 hard-exploration games tested. Montezuma 43,791 with the downscaled representation (1,731,645 with domain knowledge); Pitfall 102,571 with domain knowledge. The exploration phase found superhuman trajectories on all 55 Atari games. — [Ecoffet et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2004.12919)
- "Policy-based Go-Explore" replaces simulator restore with a goal-conditioned PPO policy that returns to the cell, so resets are "not a requirement". — [Ecoffet et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2004.12919)
- Limits: Phase 1 depends on a deterministic, resettable simulator. Cell representations can be domain-specific. Robustification is computationally expensive (the reason the study covered only 11 games). — [Ecoffet et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2004.12919); [Tavakoli et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1811.11298) notes "the criterion used to identify promising states is rather domain-specific".

**Backward algorithm (Salimans & Chen 2018). Trained from a human demonstration, so out of scope as a training method; mechanism listed only because Go-Explore reuses it on the agent's own trajectories.**
- Starts PPO episodes at a reset point τ (sampled from {τ−D … τ}) on a trajectory. τ moves earlier once at least 20% of rollout workers match the trajectory's return (1024 workers). The policy's RNN state is warmed up by replaying K trajectory actions before control starts. Score: 74,500 on Montezuma's Revenge. "Such a curriculum [was] vitally important for deriving any benefit." — [Salimans & Chen](https://arxiv.org/abs/1812.03381)

**Restarts for credit assignment rather than exploration (agent's own states)**
- TRPO "vine": generate "trunk" trajectories, choose a "rollout set" of states on them, take several actions per state with a short rollout each, and use common random numbers (CRN) to "greatly reduce the variance of the Q-value differences". This gives "much better estimates of the advantage values" at the cost of "far more calls to the simulator" and the need for arbitrary resets. — [Schulman et al., TRPO](https://arxiv.org/abs/1502.05477)
- VinePPO: for each state on a PPO trajectory, reset and sample K extra rollouts from the current policy to get an unbiased Monte Carlo V(s_t). The extra rollouts "are used exclusively for value estimation". "For any K ≥ 1, the policy gradient computed using the advantage estimator Â^MC is an unbiased estimate." Against PPO it needed 9x and 2.8x fewer gradient steps and 3.0x and 1.51x less wall-clock time (RhoMath 1.1B and DeepSeekMath 7B), despite steps being up to 5x and 2x slower. Larger K consistently raised accuracy. PPO's critic had explained variance 0.7–0.9 yet ranked candidate steps "barely" above chance. — [Kazemnejad et al., VinePPO](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.01679)

### Inferences
- The pilot's bottleneck is credit at a known, rare decision, not discovering a sparse goal, so the vine/VinePPO pattern fits better than Backplay or Florensa. Branch at each saved "target choice pending" state, roll out each candidate target (or two sampled ones) to the end of the game with shared randomness, and use the difference as the advantage. Backplay's backward schedule and Florensa's goal growth do not transfer naturally to a card game with no single goal state.
- Throughput arithmetic (estimate, not measured): full games give 0.4–1.7 target decisions per game, and a restart rollout gives exactly 1 per remainder-of-game rollout. If the remainder averages half a game, raw throughput gains only about 1.2–5x. The larger lever is variance. With paired branches from the same saved state, the same re-dealt hidden cards and the same RNG seed, Var(Q̂₁−Q̂₂) = 2σ²(1−ρ). Required visits fall by a factor of (1−ρ) on top of the K-fold averaging. ρ (the correlation of outcomes across targets from the same state) is unknown and should be measured in a 5-minute probe before committing.
- Tavakoli's 10% ratio is the only published PPO restart fraction found; it was not tuned ("to avoid the significant cost of systematic hyperparameter search").

### Gaps
- None of these papers reports speed-ups as a single "x-fold" factor except VinePPO; Tavakoli and Florensa give only curves, and Backplay gives tables at a fixed budget.
- Backplay's Theorem 3.1 statement was cut off in text extraction; only the "factor of M" conclusion was readable.
- TRPO's table of vine hyperparameters (rollouts per state, rollout length) did not extract.
- No restart-distribution study in a card game or other imperfect-information game was found (see §4).

## 3. Bias: restarts weighted toward "rare decision pending" states; how value and policy-gradient estimates are biased, and how the bias is corrected or bounded

### Takeaway
If the restored state is the full Markov state (hidden cards and any policy memory included) and the policy then acts as usual, restarts do not bias the per-state quantities Q^π(s,a), V^π(s) or A^π(s,a); they change only which states are weighted in the loss. In the tabular case or with enough capacity, the optimum is unchanged. With shared parameters, the fitted value and the policy trade accuracy toward oversampled states ("unsought learning bias"). This bias can be removed exactly in two ways. (a) Keep on-policy state weights and use the restart rollouts only to reduce the variance of the advantage at states that occurred on-policy (VinePPO/vine: unbiased for any K). (b) Importance-weight restart-derived samples by d_D(s)/μ(s), which is known when restart states are sampled from on-policy visits with a known weight w(s). A mixing fraction β bounds the deviation instead of removing it.

### Cited Findings
- Tabular case: by the principle of optimality, starting in extra states lets the agent "better optimise for the designated set by better optimising for the states that are reachable". With approximation, "changing the initial-state distribution modifies the learning objective for approximate prediction". The value loss L(w) = Σ_s μ(s) Σ_a π(a|s)[q_π(s,a) − q̂(s,a|w)]² is weighted by the state distribution, so the restart distribution decides where the critic is accurate. The same holds for policy-gradient control. — [Tavakoli et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1811.11298)
- Mitigation: use "a distribution of initial states whose support contains and spans beyond that of the environment's initial-state distribution" together with a parameterisation with enough capacity. "It may be appropriate to accept the cost of potentially introducing a learning bias in order to facilitate learning." — [Tavakoli et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1811.11298)
- Prioritising which state to restart from "does not introduce a learning bias in the way that prioritisation in the context of replay does … transitions are always sampled from the environment, not replayed … we do not need importance sampling corrections." (This holds for per-state dynamics; the state weighting of the objective still changes, as above.) — [Tavakoli et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1811.11298)
- "Any optimal policy simultaneously maximizes both η_μ and η_D", but within a restricted class the η_μ-maximiser may be poor under η_D. — [Kakade & Langford 2002](https://hunch.net/~jl/projects/aoarl/Final.tex_back)
- Bellman & Dreyfus: "there exists a single policy π* that is simultaneously optimal for all starting states". With function approximation, error is governed by the "transfer error" under the comparator's distribution. — [Agarwal et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1908.00261)
- Unbiased variance reduction: VinePPO replaces the critic-based advantage with K reset rollouts. "For any K ≥ 1, the policy gradient computed using the advantage estimator Â^MC is an unbiased estimate of the gradient of expected return." The extra rollouts are used only for value estimation, not as policy-gradient samples. VinePPO's values were unbiased, while PPO's value network "exhibits biased value predictions". — [VinePPO](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.01679)
- Vine sampling picks rollout-set states from on-policy trunk trajectories, so the state weighting stays on-policy and only the Q estimates gain extra samples. — [TRPO](https://arxiv.org/abs/1502.05477)
- CPI's estimator samples the state from d_{π,μ} and the action uniformly, then corrects with the action importance weight n_a(π′ − π). Importance weighting over whole trajectories to reach rare goal states is "not feasible" because the weights become exponentially large. — [Kakade & Langford 2002](https://hunch.net/~jl/projects/aoarl/Final.tex_back)
- (Snippet only) Off-policy state-distribution correction estimates the ratio of state distributions directly, because per-step importance ratios have variance that grows "exponentially with the problem horizon". — [Liu et al. 2019, arXiv 1904.08473](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1904.08473v1)
- (Snippet only) A 2025 analysis argues that standard PPO already samples states from the undiscounted on-policy distribution rather than the discounted one the theory assumes, making many policy-gradient methods "effectively biased stochastic gradient methods". It proposes reweighting (DisPPO). This places restart bias next to a mismatch that ordinary PPO already carries. — [arXiv 2503.22244](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.22244v1)
- Related design result: Robust PLR showed that training only on replayed (prioritised) levels, with a stop-gradient on freshly sampled ones, makes the curriculum "provably robust" at Nash equilibrium (a minimax-regret guarantee). That is a deliberate change of objective, not an unbiasedness result. — [Jiang et al., Replay-Guided Adversarial Environment Design](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.02439)

### Inferences
- **Value function.** If the critic is trained on restart rollouts that start at rare-decision states, V̂ at those states becomes more accurate, which is the goal. Capacity shifts toward those states, by a weight ratio of μ(s)/d_D(s). When the critic is fit only on on-policy-weighted data, or restart samples get weight d_D/μ, the fitted critic matches the on-policy objective. Per-state targets are unbiased either way, provided the restored state is complete (§4).
- **Policy gradient: three options from least to most bias.** (1) *Vine/VinePPO (unbiased).* Sample branch states from current on-policy games (e.g., every rare-decision state that occurred), run K rollouts per candidate action, average them into Â at that state, and give that state its normal on-policy weight. Do not add the restart rollouts' later states to the PPO batch. (2) *Importance-weighted restarts (unbiased, more variance).* If restart states are sampled from on-policy visits with probability ∝ d_D(s)·w(s), for example w = 1 when a target choice is pending and 0 otherwise, then the ratio d_D(s)/μ_R(s) ∝ 1/w(s) is known in closed form. No density estimation is needed for the branch state. The downstream states of a restart rollout have no simple ratio, which is why option (1) drops them. (3) *Plain mixing (biased but bounded).* Train on restart episodes as ordinary PPO data at fraction β. The objective becomes (1−β)η_D + βη_R. If π̂ maximises the mixture and π_D maximises η_D in the same class, then η_D(π_D) − η_D(π̂) ≤ (β/(1−β))·(η_R(π̂) − η_R(π_D)) ≤ (β/(1−β))·(value range). With β = 0.1 and outcomes in [−1, 1], that is ≤ 0.22 in the worst case. This is a loose bound, derived here from linearity and not taken from a paper.
- PPO-specific: restart episodes have a different start-of-episode state, so GAE and advantage normalisation run per batch. Mixing restart and on-policy samples in one normalisation changes the advantage scale. Keeping the restart branch advantages separate (option 1) avoids this.
- Evan's "self-discovery" rule: choosing restart states by "Computer Search / Item Finder pending" is a hand-picked, card-specific criterion. A generic criterion is closer to the rule, for example inverse visit count of each decision type, high critic TD error (Tavakoli's prioritised restart), or high variance of Â across branches. It would find these decisions without naming the cards.

### Gaps
- I found no paper that measures the bias of a restart distribution empirically (on-policy return of a policy trained with versus without restarts, at matched capacity). The evidence is theoretical (CPI, Agarwal) or curve-level (Tavakoli).
- I found no published mixing schedule beyond a fixed 10% (Tavakoli) and Backplay's hand-set windows. An annealed β → 0, which would recover the exact objective at the end, is a natural option, but I found no source that tests it.

## 4. Hidden information: restarting from belief states or determinised states in imperfect-information games

### Takeaway
In imperfect-information games the value of an information state depends on the posterior over hidden cards, and that posterior depends on the other player's policy (ReBeL, SPARTA). A restart is unbiased for value targets only if hidden cards are drawn from that policy-conditioned posterior. For a saved state from a real self-play game, the cards actually dealt are one exact sample from it (for the policies that played that game). Re-dealing the opponent's hand uniformly over all hands consistent with public information ignores what the opponent's actions reveal, and so biases values, in the spirit of PIMC's known flaws. Cards that no player has information about can be freely reshuffled. Recurrent policy state must also be restored consistently with the re-deal.

### Cited Findings
- "Unlike perfect-information games and single-agent settings, the value of an action may depend on the probability it is chosen. Thus, a state defined only by the sequence of actions and observations does not have a unique value." ReBeL therefore works with public belief states (PBSs). A subgame rooted at a PBS is played by first sampling a history "from the joint probability distribution of the PBS, and then the game proceeds as it would in the original game". V_i(β) = Σ_h p(h|β)v_i(h). — [Brown et al., ReBeL (NeurIPS 2020)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2007.13544)
- DeepStack's PBS value function "was trained not through self-play RL, but rather by generating random PBSs, including random probability distributions". This is a synthetic restart distribution over belief states, with no human data. (DeepStack's sample counts were not checked.) — [ReBeL](https://arxiv.org/abs/2007.13544)
- SPARTA keeps exact beliefs over trajectories, since in card games "the number of states that have non-zero probability conditional on a player's observations is often much smaller". Each belief update "consists of two updates: one based on the partner's known policy", that is, conditioning on what the partner's policy would have done. It samples states from these beliefs and runs rollouts. Result: 24.61/25 in 2-player Hanabi (previous best 24.08). — [Lerer et al., SPARTA (AAAI 2020)](https://arxiv.org/abs/1912.02318)
- Learned Belief Search replaces exact beliefs with an auto-regressive belief model trained by supervised learning on the agent's own self-play data (it predicts the other players' private cards). It reaches "55% ~ 91% of the benefit of exact search while reducing compute requirements by 35.8× ~ 4.6×". Practical catch: "since in Dec-POMDPs policies condition on full AOHs (typically implemented via RNNs) the games have to be replayed from the beginning for each of the sampled [trajectories] to obtain the correct hidden state". — [Hu et al., LBS (ICML 2021)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2106.09086)
- The backward algorithm warms up the policy's RNN state by replaying K trajectory actions before handing control to the agent. — [Salimans & Chen](https://arxiv.org/abs/1812.03381)
- Determinisation (PIMC) has two known flaws. Strategy fusion: the solver "plays different Max moves in the different worlds of an information set", whereas a real player must play one move across all of them. Non-locality: a locally optimal move is not the best one to back up globally (Frank & Basin, cited there). — [Cazenave et al., Optimizing αµ](https://arxiv.org/abs/2101.12639)
- (Snippet only) In Gin Rummy, a Monte Carlo search baseline "graded fairly (hidden cards re-dealt)" is weaker than trained agents, while an oracle that sees hidden cards "reaches far higher". This shows how much leaked hidden information inflates results. — [arXiv 2607.06854 abstract](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2607.06854.md)
- (Snippet only) In Skat, a neural network predicting card locations from the move history samples "more realistic" hidden states than uniform deals. Whether that network was trained on human games was not checked. — [arXiv 1903.09604](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1903.09604.pdf)

### Inferences
- **Strategy fusion does not arise** when the pilot acts from its own observation in the restarted game, as in normal self-play. It arises only when a search or oracle sees the determinised world. The relevant risk for restarts is the **posterior mismatch**.
- **Pokémon TCG specifics (my reasoning, to verify against the simulator):** (a) Shuffled deck order and face-down prizes that no player has looked at are uniform from both players' views, so they can be reshuffled together without leaking anything. (b) The opponent's hand is correlated with the opponent's past choices, such as not attaching Energy or not playing a Supporter. Keeping the actual hand from the saved game is an exact posterior sample, while a uniform re-deal is biased. (c) A deck search (Computer Search, Item Finder targets, Pokédex-style looks) reveals deck contents to the searching player. After that, that player knows by elimination which cards are in the prizes. A re-deal that moves cards between deck and prizes would contradict that player's information set, so re-deal only within each player's set of cards that are still indistinguishable to them. (d) If the opponent's hand is re-dealt, a fresh hand must also be consistent with the opponent's own private knowledge, which uniform re-dealing does not guarantee.
- **Policy-conditioned posteriors go stale in self-play.** A saved state's real hidden cards are an exact posterior sample only for the policies that played that game. If the pool holds states from older opponents, the implied posterior is outdated. Refreshing the pool from recent games keeps this small (§5).
- **Recurrent or history-based pilots:** if the pilot encodes history (an RNN or game log), the restored state must include both players' memory, rebuilt by replaying the saved action history (LBS, backward algorithm). A pilot that sees only the current board and deck, like the deck-agnostic board encoder, needs only the game state restored.
- **Cheapest unbiased choice:** branch from the saved state with the opponent's hand and all player-known cards kept as in the real game, and reshuffle only cards unknown to both players. Use different reshuffles across rollouts but the same reshuffle across the candidate targets inside one rollout, for the CRN pairing in §2.

### Gaps
- I found no RL paper that restarts self-play training episodes from mid-game determinised states in a card game and measures the effect. The closest are search methods (SPARTA, LBS, PIMC) and DeepStack's synthetic PBS training.
- Exact posterior sampling over the opponent's hand given their policy (SPARTA-style) may be too expensive in Pokémon TCG, where hands and decks are larger than in Hanabi. No source measured this.

## 5. Practical recipes: restart fraction, choosing and refreshing the pool, avoiding overfitting to a fixed pool

### Takeaway
Published working settings: about 10% of transitions from restarts (Tavakoli); a buffer of about 20,000 recent states with mild prioritisation (α = 0.4) and short truncated restart rollouts with bootstrapping; a mix of new and replayed starts (200 new + 100 old in Florensa); a filter for states of intermediate difficulty (0.1 < P(success) < 0.9); and, from PLR, staleness-aware sampling so stale priorities are refreshed. To avoid overfitting: keep the true start distribution in the mix, cap how many restart states come from one source episode, prefer recent states, and move curricula slowly rather than quickly. For a credit-assignment use (not exploration), branch rollouts from on-policy states of the current batch, which need no persistent pool and cannot go stale.

### Cited Findings
- Fraction and length: 10% of transitions from restarts, restart rollouts capped at T_aug = 10 steps with bootstrapping from partial episodes (Pardo et al. cited), and buffer size 20,000. The paper did not find uniform or prioritised restart to be consistently better: prioritised won on HalfCheetah and uniform on Humanoid. — [Tavakoli et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1811.11298)
- Pool diversity: nested parent/sub-episode storage caps sub-episodes per parent, so the pool cannot collapse onto one easy goal. — [Tavakoli et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1811.11298)
- Difficulty filter: train on starts where the agent "reaches the goal sometimes, but not always" (R_min = 0.1, R_max = 0.9). Estimate success from the training rollouts themselves instead of paying for separate evaluation. Replay old good starts (N_old = 100 alongside N_new = 200) to prevent forgetting. — [Florensa et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1707.05300)
- Schedule: advancing too fast hurts and advancing too slowly does not. Adaptive success thresholds were "too slow". A 20% "workers matching the trajectory" threshold worked for the backward algorithm. — [Backplay](https://arxiv.org/abs/1807.06919); [Salimans & Chen](https://arxiv.org/abs/1812.03381)
- Staleness: PLR scores levels by a learning-potential signal (e.g., mean |GAE|) and mixes score-based and staleness-based sampling, P_replay = (1−ρ)P_S + ρP_C, with staleness c − C_i. Gains appear "only … when P_replay considers both level scores and staleness (0 < ρ < 1)". Best Procgen settings were rank prioritisation with β = 0.1 and ρ = 0.1, giving significant gains in mean normalised test return and sample efficiency over uniform sampling (PPO, 25M steps, 200 levels). — [Jiang et al., Prioritized Level Replay (ICML 2021)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.03934)
- Variance reduction per restart: several actions per branch state with common random numbers (vine), and K = 1, 3, 9 auxiliary rollouts with accuracy rising in K (VinePPO). Each iteration is slower (up to 2–5x), but there are fewer iterations overall. — [TRPO](https://arxiv.org/abs/1502.05477); [VinePPO](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.01679)
- Generalisation: Backplay agents trained on 100 Pommerman maps won 416/1000 games on held-out maps. Maze agents trained on fewer maps did not generalise, which the authors attribute to "not including enough mazes". — [Backplay](https://arxiv.org/abs/1807.06919)

### Inferences
- **Recipe for the pilot's rare target decision (inference; sizes to be set by a 5-minute pilot):**
  1. During normal PPO self-play, snapshot the full game state and RNG at every pending target choice (any rare decision type, by a generic visit-count criterion).
  2. In the same iteration, branch each snapshot. Keep the real hidden cards and reshuffle only cards unknown to both players. Play each candidate target (or 2–4 sampled candidates) to the end of the game, K times, sharing the reshuffle and seed across candidates within each k.
  3. Use the branch-averaged advantage for that decision only, at its on-policy weight (the unbiased vine/VinePPO estimator), and feed nothing downstream into the PPO batch.
  4. Measure ρ between candidates' outcomes and the per-decision standard error. Stop if the standard error is not falling faster than with plain PPO.
  Because the snapshots come from the current batch, there is no stale pool and no overfitting to a fixed set.
- If a persistent pool is used (Tavakoli-style restarts for exploration), keep β ≈ 0.1. Draw restart states only from the last N iterations' games, add a staleness term (PLR, ρ ≈ 0.1), cap restarts per source game, and keep at least 1−β of episodes from fresh deals so the true-start support is retained.
- A Florensa-style difficulty filter has a natural analogue: prefer branch states where the candidate targets' estimated values differ, or where the outcome is uncertain (win probability between 0.1 and 0.9). Decisions in already-decided games carry no signal.

### Gaps
- I found no source that tunes the restart fraction β systematically. Tavakoli fixed it at 0.1 without a search, and the others use schedules rather than fractions.
- I found no published guidance on pool size or refresh rate specifically for self-play, where the opponent policy, and hence the hidden-card posterior, drifts.
- The ρ (outcome correlation across targets from one state) that determines CRN gains is game-specific. No Pokémon TCG or card-game figure exists, so it must be measured.
