# Prioritised / stratified sampling of rare decision types inside on-policy actor-critic and PPO

Context: self-play PPO for Pokémon TCG. Sub-decisions such as the Computer Search fetch/discard choice or the Item Finder target appear 0.4-1.7 times per game (out of about 150 decisions). Each has a per-decision value gap of about 0.02 of the ±1 result. A 30-minute run sees about 10k of them, against about 290k needed for single-visit credit (caller's figures).

Reading-depth tags: **[full]** means the full text was read, through local PDF extraction or ar5iv/HTML fetch. **[abstract]** means only the abstract or landing page was read. **[snippet]** means only a search-result summary was seen. **[memory]** means the point comes from my own background knowledge and was not re-read in this session.

## 1. Prioritised experience replay and its IS correction: what carries over to on-policy / near-on-policy methods (PPO + small replay, IMPALA/V-trace, ACER)? Any results applying prioritisation to PPO?

### Takeaway
PER's mechanism carries over directly to sampling within a PPO buffer: sample with probability P(i) and weight each sample by (N·P(i))^-β. With β=1 the expected gradient is the same as uniform sampling, so the only gain is lower variance. With β<1 it becomes state reweighting (see §2). The published evidence for prioritised replay inside actor-critic is mixed to negative. LASER found no gain over uniform replay on DMLab-30, and ACER plus prioritised replay did worse than plain A2C. The positive PPO results (PTR-PPO, SIL) are small-scale, and in SIL the gain comes from replaying only positive-advantage samples, not from the prioritisation itself.

### Cited Findings
- PER (Schaul et al. 2016): P(i) = p_i^α / Σ p_k^α with IS weights w_i = (N·P(i))^-β, normalised by max w, and β annealed toward 1 by the end of training. Proportional variant: α≈0.6, β₀≈0.4. **[memory]**, consistent with the tutorial summary in [apxml PER page](https://apxml.com/courses/advanced-reinforcement-learning/chapter-2-deep-q-networks/prioritized-experience-replay) **[snippet]**.
- LASER (Schmitt, Hessel, Simonyan, ICML 2020) uses **uniform** replay. Prioritised replay showed "little benefit of actor critic prioritized replay on DMLab-30". Its best prioritised agent, with full IS correction (β=1), "performs no higher than with uniform replay" (Appendix A.2; priorities from critic loss; LSTM states not refreshed). The authors chose uniform replay because it "is instead almost parameter-free". — [LASER, ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1909.11583) **[full]**
- Self-Imitation Learning (Oh et al. 2018), 49 Atari games at 50M steps, median human-normalised score: **A2C 96.1%, ACPER (A2C + ACER-style off-policy AC with prioritised replay, priority (R−V)+) 46.8%, A2C+SIL 138.7%**. ACPER was "very unstable on many other games". Prioritised off-policy actor-critic therefore did worse than no replay, while the same prioritisation feeding a clipped positive-advantage imitation loss did better. — [SIL paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1806.05635) **[full]**
- SIL settings: priority ∝ (R−V_θ(s))+, exponent 0.6, IS bias correction β = 0.4 (0.1 in the hard-exploration runs), buffer 10^5, 4 SIL updates per A2C iteration with batch 512. PPO+SIL (MuJoCo): 10 SIL updates per batch, SIL loss weight 0.1, buffer 50k. PPO+SIL improved Swimmer, Walker2d and Ant. — [SIL paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1806.05635) **[full]**
- SIL failure mode: the agent "sometimes gets stuck at a sub-optimal policy" (James Bond, Star Gunner) through "excessive exploitation at the early stage". The fix was to reduce SIL updates per iteration or the SIL weight later in training. — [SIL paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1806.05635) **[full]**
- PTR-PPO (Liang et al. 2021) prioritises whole trajectories by max |1-step GAE|, mean |1-step GAE|, or |normalised return|. It truncates the importance weight (ACER-style, cap tied to ε=0.2) and applies **no PER-style β correction** for the non-uniform sampling. On 6 Atari games, 40k steps and 5 seeds, it "outperforms or matches" PPO and ACER on all 6 (best variant vs PPO: Atlantis about 13×, UpNDown about 4.3×, Qbert about 2.3×, Bowling about 1.9×, NameThisGame about 1.25×, Breakout about 1.2×). Priority memory size mattered: 256 beat 32 and 1024. Caveats: a very short horizon, and a PPO Atlantis score of 2000±0 that looks suspect. — [PTR-PPO, ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2112.03798) **[full via fetch summary]**
- InPTR (follow-up by the same group) uses the same three priority metrics with truncated IS weights inside PPO. — [ACM DL](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3719545.3721109) **[snippet]**
- Libardi et al. (ICML 2021) replay the agent's own past trajectories in PPO, ranked by reward and value, with IS correction. Only part of each batch is on-policy. — [PMLR](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v139/libardi21a/libardi21a.pdf) **[snippet]** (the method also uses a demonstration; only the self-replay part is relevant here)
- The PPO probability ratio is the same quantity as the IS weight, so replaying older trajectories needs that ratio corrected relative to the behaviour policy. — [search summary of PTR-PPO/Libardi](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/356841783_PTR-PPO_Proximal_Policy_Optimization_with_Prioritized_Trajectory_Replay) **[snippet]**
- ACER does **not** use prioritised replay. Prioritised DQN appears only as a comparison curve. — [ACER, ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1611.01224) **[full]**
- Rare-event importance sampling (Frank, Mannor & Precup, ICML 2008) assumes a simulator in which the rare event's probability can be set. It oversamples the event and corrects TD updates with IS. REASA adapts the sampling rate to minimise variance. Convergence is proven for tabular and linear TD, with a bias-variance analysis for the tabular case. **Policy evaluation only**, and the rare event is an *environment* event, not an agent decision. — [Frank et al. 2008](https://cs.mcgill.ca/~jfrank8/pubs/icml08rareevents.pdf) **[full]**

### Inferences
- Inside a single PPO iteration, every sample is visited each epoch. Prioritised or stratified minibatching with full IS correction (β=1) therefore has the same expected epoch gradient as uniform sampling. It only ensures that every minibatch contains some rare-type samples, which lowers minibatch gradient variance for the rare head and gives Adam steady gradients. With β<1 or no correction, it is mathematically the same as per-type loss upweighting (§2).
- The best-supported way to "prioritise" without bias is not to resample existing data but to **generate more visits**, the Frank et al. approach applied to the agent's own decision points. Examples: reset or branch self-play from saved states just before a Computer Search or Item Finder decision, or replay several candidate answers from the same state. This is a change to the state distribution, which on-policy PG tolerates (see §2), not an off-policy action correction.
- The SIL result (prioritisation helps only when the loss is restricted to (R−V)+) is a warning for card games. With a ±1 outcome and a 0.02 gap, (R−V)+ selects lucky games far more than good sub-decisions, so SIL-style replay on rare decisions would mostly imitate draw luck. AlphaStar's UPGO bootstraps from V(s_{t+1}) when the behaviour action was worse than average, which is a lower-variance variant (§3).

### Gaps
- No paper found that applies prioritisation **by action/decision type** (rather than by TD error or return) inside PPO.
- PTR-PPO and InPTR results are on 6 Atari games at 40k steps. No large-scale replication found.
- LASER gives no numeric gap between prioritised and uniform replay, only "no higher".

## 2. Per-decision-type loss weighting or stratified minibatches: is the PG still unbiased? Effect on effective learning rate and stability

### Takeaway
A weight that depends only on the **state** (the decision type is a property of the prompt state) leaves the per-state improvement direction unchanged. Only the state weighting of the objective changes, which is the same kind of "bias" every practical PG method already carries. A weight that depends on the **chosen action** is genuinely biased, because the baseline term no longer cancels. Under Adam and PPO clipping, upweighting a rare head's loss does much less than it appears to: the information bottleneck (signal-to-noise at 10k samples) is unchanged.

### Cited Findings
- The commonly used PG update (outer γ^t dropped) "need not equal the gradient of any function". There is a counterexample in which it converges to a "globally pessimal" policy. In practice, state weighting in PG updates is already not the textbook d^π. — [Nota & Thomas, "Is the Policy Gradient a Gradient?"](https://arxiv.org/abs/1906.07073) **[abstract]**
- PopArt states that "the saliency of a task for the agent increases with the scale of the returns observed in that task". Tasks with larger or denser returns dominate the shared updates, which motivates per-task rescaling of gradient share. — [PopArt multi-task, ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1809.04474) **[full]**
- Per-minibatch advantage normalisation is a standard PPO detail. Its effect is task-dependent: it helped on some MuJoCo tasks and hurt on others. — [Revisiting Design Choices in PPO](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2009.10897) **[snippet]**; [Hyperparameter-sensitivity paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2412.07165) **[snippet]**
- Batch-size invariance: PPO's update-size control does not scale like SGD's learning rate when the batch changes. Decoupling the "proximal" policy (which sets the trust region) from the behaviour policy (used for IS) fixes this. — [Hilton, Cobbe, Schulman 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.00641) **[abstract]**

### Inferences (derivations are mine, not cited)
- **State-dependent weight w(s)** (for example ×10 on every "choose Computer Search target" state): E_{a~π}[w(s)(Q−b(s))∇log π] = w(s)·Σ_a ∇π(a|s)Q(s,a). The baseline term still vanishes and the per-state direction is the true one, scaled. With fully separate parameters per decision type, the fixed points are unchanged and the effect is a per-type step size. With a shared trunk, the update is the gradient of a surrogate with state distribution ∝ d^π(s)w(s). It is "biased" with respect to J in the same way as dropping γ^t or normalising advantages, so it is benign if w is bounded.
- **Action-dependent weight w(s,a)** (for example upweighting the sample whenever the agent *chose* to play Computer Search at a main-phase decision): the baseline term −b(s)Σ_a w(s,a)∇π(a|s) ≠ 0. The update then depends on the baseline and systematically pushes toward or away from upweighted actions, depending on the sign of the baseline error. This is real bias. Avoid it, or correct it with a full IS ratio.
- **Effective learning rate under Adam:** Adam's per-parameter step m/√v is invariant to a constant rescaling of the gradient **[memory: Kingma & Ba 2015]**. If the rare sub-decision has its own head or pointer parameters, multiplying its loss by w barely changes how fast that head moves. It mostly raises the rare type's share of the **shared-trunk** gradient, which is where interference and instability come from. Under global-norm gradient clipping, upweighted noisy rare-type gradients can also shrink every other type's step.
- **Under PPO clipping:** w scales the gradient inside [1−ε, 1+ε] but not the clip range. Upweighted rare-state ratios hit the clip in fewer epochs, after which their gradient is zero. Per-iteration policy movement at rare states is therefore capped by ε whatever w is.
- **Information bound (estimate, not measured):** weighting scales signal and noise together. Distinguishing a gap Δ with per-sample noise σ needs n ∝ σ²/Δ². With σ≈1 (±1 outcome) and Δ=0.02, roughly 2·(2σ/Δ)² ≈ 20k samples per compared option for a 2-sigma call. No reweighting or resampling scheme changes n. Only **lower σ** (a better baseline or critic, afterstate values, common-random-number branching) or **more visits** (state-reset or branching, §1) does. A critic that explains 75% of outcome variance halves σ and cuts n by about 4×.
- **Stratified minibatches** (guarantee k rare samples per minibatch, weighted so each epoch's expectation is unchanged) are the low-risk version. They add no bias and smooth Adam's intermittent-gradient dynamics for the rare head.

### Gaps
- No empirical paper found that measures per-action-type loss upweighting in PPO and reports its effect on stability or sample counts.
- The Adam-invariance argument assumes the rare head's parameters receive gradient only from rare-type samples. With shared embeddings or pointer keys the picture is mixed. Not tested in any source found.

## 3. Separate heads, separate value baselines, or separate normalisation per decision type: does it help rare heads learn?

### Takeaway
The large game systems factorise the action into heads and give each head its own IS ratio. AlphaStar does this specifically to stop trace cutting in a huge action space; Honor of Kings sums one clipped term per head. Per-task normalisation (PopArt) gave large gains when tasks differed in **return scale**. That does not apply here: all decision types share the same ±1 return, so the problem is frequency and gap size, not scale. Per-type normalisation should therefore centre the advantage (subtract a type-specific baseline) but not inflate it by 1/σ.

### Cited Findings
- AlphaStar: "off-policy correction methods like V-trace can be inefficient in large, structured action spaces ... because distinct actions can result in similar (or even identical) behaviour". "To mitigate early trace cutting due to the large action space, we assume independence between the action type, delay, and all other arguments, and so update them separately." The policy uses V-trace, while the value uses TD(λ) **without** off-policy correction. UPGO updates toward ρ_t(G^U_t − V(s_t))∇log π, with ρ_t = min(π/π_behaviour, 1) and a one-step action-value estimate Q = r_t + V(s_{t+1}). Ablation (Fig. 3I, average win rate vs fixed opponents): **V-trace 49% → +TD(λ) 73% → +UPGO 82%**. Fig. 3K: value function without opponent info 22% vs with 82%. Caveat: AlphaStar also used human data (supervised initialisation, KL to the supervised policy, human z-statistics). Only the RL mechanisms are relevant here. — [AlphaStar paper (unformatted)](https://storage.googleapis.com/deepmind-media/research/alphastar/AlphaStar_unformatted.pdf) **[full]**
- Honor of Kings (Ye et al., AAAI 2020), "control dependency decoupling": "each label in an action" is treated independently. The PPO objective sums one clipped term per head, each with its own ratio π_θ(a⁽ⁱ⁾|s)/π_old(a⁽ⁱ⁾|s) and a **shared** advantage. Dual-clip PPO adds a floor c·Â (c=3) for Â<0, which bounds large ratios on off-policy data. No ablation of decoupling or dual-clip was reported. The action-mask ablation reached 50.5% win rate vs base with training time cut from 80h to 65h. — [Honor of Kings, ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1912.09729) **[full]**
- PopArt-IMPALA keeps per-task value heads and per-task statistics (μ_i, σ_i). **Both value targets and policy-gradient advantages** are normalised per task, while the policy is shared. Median human-normalised score on Atari-57 (clipped rewards): **about 110% vs about 60% for IMPALA**. Unclipped: about 101% vs about 0%. DMLab-30 mean capped score: 72.8% vs 58.4% (test). The paper gives no analysis of the bias from per-task normalisation. — [PopArt, ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1809.04474) **[full]**
- In a multi-reward setting, normalising each advantage component separately "would erase their relative scale". The authors instead centre each component and apply one global scale. — [Multi-objective robot RL](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2509.14816) **[snippet]**
- Flavin & Sen (arXiv, 25 June 2026), 220 configurations across PPO, SAC and DQN: they propose VDN-PPO and PPO-MIX, which use a "branching critic to assign credit to multi-headed PPO", and these outperform the other PPO factorisations tested. Autoregressive action factorisation reached the highest performance overall. — [arXiv 2606.26574](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.26574) **[abstract]** (the search snippet adds that heads are weighted by advantage range **[snippet]**)
- Value training tolerates far more sample reuse than policy training (PPG: "Value function optimization often tolerates a significantly higher level of sample reuse than policy optimization"). — [PPG](https://arxiv.org/html/2009.04416v1) **[full via fetch summary]**

### Inferences
- For the Computer Search and Item Finder sub-decisions, the analogue of AlphaStar's independent-head treatment is to give the sub-decision's IS ratio its own clip and not multiply it into the parent "play card" ratio. With a joint ratio, a stale parent head cuts the trace for the child.
- The highest-leverage "separate baseline" is an **action-dependent critic for the sub-decision**, Q(s, candidate) via the afterstate V(s′) after the fetched card enters the hand, as in AlphaStar's one-step Q = r + V(s_{t+1}). The advantage then becomes Q(s,a) − Σ_a π(a|s)Q(s,a), computed for all candidates (an "all-action" or mean-actor-critic gradient, **[memory: Allen et al. 2017]**). This removes most of the ±1 game noise from the sub-decision's gradient, which is the σ in §2's n ∝ σ²/Δ².
- Per-type advantage normalisation: subtracting a per-type mean is safe and helps. Dividing by a per-type std computed from a few rare samples inflates noise, and since the types share the ±1 return the scale is already comparable. Prefer one global scale (as in the multi-objective paper).

### Gaps
- OpenAI Five's per-head treatment (shared vs per-head ratio, masking unused argument heads) was not checked in this session.
- No source found that ablates per-head vs joint ratios specifically for **rarely active** heads.
- The full Flavin & Sen paper (per-head advantage weighting, results) was not read.

## 4. Off-policy reuse of rare-decision samples across iterations (keeping them longer than one PPO iteration) with IS correction or clipping: evidence and failure modes

### Takeaway
Reusing data from the last few policies with per-sample behaviour ratios, a tighter clip and a TV/KL step control is well supported: GePPO, ACER, LASER and decoupled PPO. The documented failure modes are:
- V-trace policy gradients on replay alone can converge to the wrong policy, so some on-policy data must be mixed in.
- Naive sharing of stale data is worse than no replay.
- Long-horizon trace corrections get cut early.

For rare sub-decisions, the safest reuse is for the **value or Q target**, recomputed with the current critic at the decision's afterstate, rather than reusing stale Monte-Carlo returns.

### Cited Findings
- LASER **Proposition 2**: "given the optimal value function V*, the V-trace policy gradient does not converge to a locally optimal π*" for all behaviour policies μ. Counterexample: Q* = (2,5), μ = (0.9,0.1), π = (0.5,0.5), with distorted values (2,1), so the wrong action is selected. **Proposition 3**: mixing a fraction α of on-policy data preserves the argmax when α/(1−α) exceeds a bound involving the Q-gap and d^μ/d^π. α = 1/8 sufficed on DMLab-30. Replay ratios of 1/2, 3/4 and 7/8 trained stably, with higher ratios more data-efficient. 100% replay ("LASER 87.5% replay" with all samples from replay) caused "a significant performance decrease". Naive sharing "is worse than the no-replay baseline". A KL trust region rejects samples with KL(π‖π̃_μ) ≥ b from all losses. Filtering by small π/μ "would introduce an additional bias". Raising V-trace's ρ̄ "is not a solution". Atari: 423% median at 60M frames per agent (448% at 200M), vs >3,000M steps for R2D2 to reach 400%. — [LASER, ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1909.11583) **[full]**
- ACER uses truncated IS ρ̄ = min(c, ρ) plus a bias-correction term active when ρ > c, so the full gradient stays unbiased (c=10 on Atari, c=5 on continuous control). Retrace gives the Q targets. Replay steps per on-policy step are Poisson with mean r ∈ {0,1,4,8}. With the trust region, performance increases with replay ratio with diminishing returns, and a ratio of 4 is the recommended trade-off. Removing truncation-with-bias-correction mattered mainly on Humanoid. — [ACER, ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1611.01224) **[full]** (no numeric table; results are in Fig. 1)
- GePPO (Queeney et al., NeurIPS 2021) reuses data from the last M=4 policies, with each sample's ratio taken against its own behaviour policy. The clip is re-centred and shrunk to ε_GePPO = ε_PPO / E[i+1] (0.2 → 0.1). Advantages come from off-policy GAE with V-trace (c̄=1, λ=0.97). An adaptive learning rate holds the estimated TV distance near ε/2. On MuJoCo (1M steps, 5 seeds) it reached PPO's final performance with **about 15-77% fewer samples** (Swimmer at about 0.23× PPO's steps, Humanoid at about 0.85×), with average performance +8% to +65%. Failure mode: PPO's own per-update TV drift grows over training (about 40% over target on Walker2d), and wide networks make clipping ineffective. — [GePPO, ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2111.00072) **[full]**
- Decoupled PPO separates the proximal policy (trust region) from the behaviour policy (IS ratios), which lets PPO "use stale data more efficiently". — [Hilton et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.00641) **[abstract]**
- P3O interleaves on-policy and off-policy updates, using the effective sample size of the IS ratios to limit behaviour-target divergence, with no extra hyperparameters, and reports reduced sample complexity on Atari and MuJoCo. — [P3O](https://arxiv.org/abs/1905.01756) **[abstract]** (no numbers seen)
- PPG keeps all states and value targets from N_π = 32 policy iterations and trains the value (and an auxiliary value head on the policy net) for E_aux = 6 epochs on them. A behaviour-cloning KL (β_clone = 1) keeps the policy fixed, and E_π = 1 policy epoch is near-optimal. PPG showed "significantly better sample efficiency than PPO in nearly every environment" on Procgen. — [PPG](https://arxiv.org/html/2009.04416v1) **[full via fetch summary]**
- AlphaStar: V-trace's "early trace cutting due to the large action space" motivated the per-head independence assumption. Values trained by TD(λ) **without** off-policy correction beat V-trace values (49% → 73% in the ablation). — [AlphaStar](https://storage.googleapis.com/deepmind-media/research/alphastar/AlphaStar_unformatted.pdf) **[full]**
- Dual-clip PPO exists because ratios on off-policy data can be huge with negative advantages, which gives unbounded variance. — [Honor of Kings](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1912.09729) **[full]**

### Inferences
- For a rare head, staleness in **policy** terms is small. The sub-policy at those states moves little per iteration (few samples, clipped), so behaviour ratios on stored rare-decision samples stay near 1 for several iterations and GePPO-style reuse is cheap. The danger is the **return**: a stored Monte-Carlo return after a Computer Search choice reflects the ~100 later decisions of an old policy. Trace corrections along that tail are cut almost immediately, which is AlphaStar's point. Recommended pattern: store (state, candidates, chosen, behaviour log-prob). On each reuse, recompute the target from the **current** critic at the one-step afterstate (UPGO/one-step Q style, or MuZero-Reanalyse style **[memory]**). Apply a GePPO-style clip relative to the behaviour policy and drop samples whose KL exceeds a threshold (LASER).
- Keep a fixed on-policy fraction in every minibatch (LASER: ≥1/8) so the rare head cannot drift onto the V-trace-biased fixed point.
- The rare-decision buffer can be large (PPG reuses 32 iterations for value training). Most of the leverage is on the value or Q side, where reuse tolerance is highest. Policy reuse should stay at about 1-4 policies back (GePPO M=4).

### Gaps
- No study found that keeps **only a subset** of transitions (by action type) longer than the rest. All reuse methods found replay whole trajectories or batches uniformly or by TD/return priority.
- No source quantified how old a stored return can be before it hurts, in a long-horizon game with a shifting downstream policy.

## 5. Quantitative results on how much these methods cut the samples needed for low-frequency actions

### Takeaway
No paper found reports sample savings specifically for low-frequency **actions** or decision types. The nearest numbers are:
- general sample-efficiency gains from replay reuse (GePPO 15-77% fewer samples; LASER's large Atari gains);
- rare-**environment-event** IS for policy evaluation (Frank et al.; APE's "orders of magnitude", seen only as a snippet);
- normalisation gains for low-salience tasks (PopArt 60% → 110% median).

None transfers directly. The binding constraint for a 0.02 gap at 10k visits is variance, which only critic quality or extra visits reduce.

### Cited Findings
- Frank et al. 2008. Random MDPs had 10 states, a rare-event probability of 0.001 and γ=0.7. TD(λ) was given 2,300 real steps per plotted "step" (≈0.9 chance of seeing a rare event), yet the IS methods (REASA, ASA) still converged faster, with lower RMSE over 70 runs. Results are reported only as figures, with no speedup factor. In a 10-city network-planning task, REASA learned to raise the link-failure sampling probability to 0.155 (≈ one failure per 54 days vs the true one per 1,460 days, an oversampling of about 27× computed from those figures). TD(λ) converged to the same value "on longer runs" with high variance. — [Frank et al. 2008](https://cs.mcgill.ca/~jfrank8/pubs/icml08rareevents.pdf) **[full]**
- APE (Xu et al.), an adversarial adaptive-IS policy evaluator for safety-critical rare events, reportedly uses "orders of magnitude fewer samples" than baselines. — [arXiv 2106.10566](https://arxiv.org/abs/2106.10566v2) **[snippet]**
- GePPO reached PPO's final MuJoCo performance with 15-77% fewer samples. — [GePPO](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2111.00072) **[full]**
- LASER: 423% Atari median at 60M frames per agent, vs >3,000M steps for R2D2 to reach 400% (different setups). — [LASER](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1909.11583) **[full]**
- PopArt: 60% → 110% Atari-57 median, by equalising each task's gradient share. — [PopArt](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1809.04474) **[full]**
- SIL: 96.1% → 138.7% median (A2C → A2C+SIL), while prioritised ACER-style replay fell to 46.8%. — [SIL](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1806.05635) **[full]**
- Prioritized Level Replay prioritises which levels (start conditions) to replay by TD-error-based "learning potential", which gives an emergent curriculum. Combined with the prior best method, it gave ">76% improvement in test return" on Procgen. — [PLR](https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.03934) **[abstract]**. That PLR uses no IS correction, because it deliberately changes the training distribution, and mixes in a staleness term is **[memory]**.

### Inferences
- Applied to this project (estimates, not measured):
  - Per-type loss weighting or stratification: about 0× reduction in the visits needed (same information).
  - Off-policy reuse of rare samples across about 4 iterations: up to about 4× more gradient passes, but no more information. At best it reaches the information limit faster.
  - A sub-decision critic that cuts per-sample σ from about 1 to about 0.5: about 4× fewer visits.
  - Branching or reset self-play at rare decision states (PLR/Frank-style start-state oversampling): visits scale with the oversampling factor. The Frank et al. network task implies about 27× is usable for evaluation.
- The two levers that change the 290k-vs-10k gap are therefore (a) a lower-variance target at the sub-decision and (b) more visits via state branching. Both are self-discovery methods: no human data or oracle.

### Gaps
- No study found that reports samples-to-learn as a function of action frequency in PPO or other actor-critic methods, with or without reweighting.
- Not read in this session: OpenAI Five's head handling; full texts of Hilton et al. 2022, P3O and PLR; Schaul et al. PER (cited from memory).
